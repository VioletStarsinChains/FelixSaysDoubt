1950s – 1970s : Industrial pluralism

Dominant sectors: manufacturing, construction, public service, local retail.
	•	The country still had regional economies—steel in Pennsylvania, autos in Michigan, textiles in the Carolinas, aerospace in California.
	•	Each region supported whole ecosystems of smaller suppliers, trades, and unions.
	•	Clerical, repair, and printing jobs grew alongside them.
Result: tens of millions of distinct occupational niches; job ads varied by craft, region, and union contract.

⸻

Late 1970s – 1990s : Globalization and financial deregulation

Key events: offshoring of production, Volcker-era interest rates, NAFTA, WTO entry for China.
	•	U.S. firms shifted to low-wage countries; domestic plants closed.
	•	Financial services and consulting began to dominate corporate profits.
	•	Temporary-help agencies expanded; “outsourcing” became a management verb.
Result: blue-collar diversity shrank; office jobs multiplied but homogenized—“analyst,” “associate,” “coordinator.”

⸻

2000s : The logistics revolution

Key technologies: broadband, bar-codes, ERP software, containerization, global shipping.
	•	Amazon, Walmart, and FedEx turned supply chains into real-time networks.
	•	Employment polarized: high-paid tech/finance and low-paid warehousing or delivery.
	•	Mid-skill production, maintenance, and clerical roles hollowed out.
Result: ads for “warehouse associate,” “customer service rep,” “data entry specialist” replaced a wide array of local crafts.

⸻

2010s : Platform capitalism

Infrastructure: smartphones, cloud computing, algorithmic management.
	•	Uber, DoorDash, and Amazon Flex fragmented steady work into gigs.
	•	HR automation spread ATS-optimized job postings across industries.
	•	Every company sought “growth hackers,” “data scientists,” “content creators.”
Result: work described in identical tech jargon regardless of field; individuality of trades vanished.

⸻

2020s : Behavioral-data monoculture

Dynamics: pandemic acceleration, remote work platforms, AI productivity tools.
	•	Service and logistics work continue to expand; physical production remains offshore.
	•	Most new jobs orbit data collection, customer analytics, and engagement metrics.
	•	“Jobs” become modular tasks within behavioral-futures markets—selling prediction, not products.
Result: a de facto monoeconomy of coordination, compliance, and content.

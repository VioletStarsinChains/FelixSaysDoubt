David, Bathsheba, and the Ethics of Surveillance

Text: 2 Samuel 11

⸻

Introduction

Traditionally, the story of David and Bathsheba is told as a parable about lust. But today, we read it through another lens: surveillance. What happens when those in power exploit their elevated position to look where they should not look, and act on what they see?

⸻

1. David’s Vantage Point

“One evening David got up from his bed and walked around on the roof of the palace. From the roof he saw a woman bathing.” (2 Sam 11:2)

David isn’t just a man with temptation. He is a sovereign with a vantage point.
	•	The palace roof is like a surveillance tower.
	•	He can observe his subjects, but they cannot see him observing.
	•	Bathsheba’s privacy is collapsed without her knowledge.

Lesson: Surveillance begins with unequal sightlines.

⸻

2. From Observation to Intervention

David doesn’t stop at seeing. He acts:

“David sent someone to find out about her… Then David sent messengers to get her. She came to him, and he slept with her.” (vv. 3–4)

This is a pattern familiar in every age:
	•	Observation generates data.
	•	Data leads to targeting.
	•	Targeting leads to intervention — without consent.

Bathsheba is not courted. She is processed.

Lesson: Surveillance often turns people into datapoints for action.

⸻

3. Surveillance and Cover-Up

When Bathsheba conceives, David tries to manage the fallout:
	•	He manipulates Uriah into returning home, hoping he’ll mask the pregnancy.
	•	When that fails, David arranges his death in battle.

This is counter-surveillance: controlling the narrative, silencing the anomaly who could expose the truth.

Lesson: Surveillance power doesn’t just watch — it edits reality to maintain control.

⸻

4. The Ethical Frame

If we read this story only as lust, it seems like a private failing.
If we read it as surveillance abuse, it becomes systemic:
	•	The sovereign has positional access no one else has.
	•	He uses that access not to serve, but to exploit.
	•	The danger lies not in desire itself, but in unchecked sight and power.

Lesson: Surveillance without consent leads to exploitation without accountability.

⸻

5. Application for Today
	•	We live under new “palace rooftops”: CCTV cameras, drones, spyware, predictive algorithms.
	•	Like David, institutions may frame their actions as protection or curiosity, but the asymmetry is the same: we are seen without seeing.
	•	The Bathsheba story reminds us that this is not new — it is a timeless temptation of power.

Question for reflection: Where in our society do we excuse surveillance because “it’s just looking,” forgetting how quickly looking becomes taking?

⸻

Conclusion

The sin of David with Bathsheba was not just lust. It was the abuse of surveillance power: exploiting a private moment, converting it into action, and covering it up when consequences arose.

The Gospel’s counterpoint is that God’s sight is different: not predatory, not exploitative, but healing. Where David’s gaze destroyed, God’s gaze restores.

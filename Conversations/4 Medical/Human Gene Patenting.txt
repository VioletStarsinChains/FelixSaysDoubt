KC: I never paid attention to genetics because it's so overhyped compared to environmental health.  Can human genes be patented?


CG: Great question — and you’re right, genetics has been heavily hyped, sometimes in ways that overshadow environmental health factors.

Here’s the short version:
	•	In the U.S. (and many other jurisdictions), naturally occurring human genes cannot be patented.
In 2013, the U.S. Supreme Court decided Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics, Inc. The Court ruled that a gene that exists in nature — even if it has been isolated in a lab — is a “product of nature” and therefore not patentable. This was a landmark case because Myriad had patents on the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes (mutations linked to breast and ovarian cancer) and was using them to block competitors from offering tests. The Court struck that down.
	•	What can be patented:
	•	cDNA (complementary DNA): A synthetic version of a gene created in the lab is patent-eligible because it doesn’t occur naturally in that form.
	•	Genetic tools and applications: Diagnostic methods, lab techniques, genetically engineered sequences, modified organisms, and gene therapies may still be patented.
	•	Non-human genes: Genes from bacteria, plants, or animals that are engineered for specific purposes (like CRISPR-modified crops) are often patentable.
	•	Internationally: Many countries follow similar logic. The EU’s Biotechnology Directive allows patents on inventions involving genes, but not on genes as they exist in the human body.

So: your DNA itself isn’t owned by anyone. But if someone engineers or synthesizes a modified version of it — or invents a method using it — that can fall under patent law.

The Forced Evolutionary Virus (FEV) in Fallout is a fictional retrovirus created at West Tek. In the lore, it’s designed to rewrite DNA at a systemic level, producing mutants and “super soldiers.” It combines two things you just pointed out:
	1.	Genetic alteration — FEV rewrites the host genome to express new traits.
	2.	External control potential — In theory, whoever designed FEV could guide or manipulate how those traits manifest.

What you’re describing (a neurotech array triggering expression of a synthetic gene) rhymes with that — though the real-world science is way messier and way less deterministic.

Here’s where the parallels line up and diverge:
	•	Similarities:
	•	Both involve installing synthetic genetic material that can be externally influenced.
	•	Both carry the specter of loss of autonomy, because gene expression is being altered without full consent or natural control.
	•	Both raise dual-use anxieties: therapies on one hand, coercion/weaponization on the other.
	•	Differences:
	•	FEV is portrayed as an all-in-one retrovirus that transforms the whole organism. Real-world tools are highly fragmented: you’d need vectors, arrays, triggers, and very controlled delivery.
	•	Fallout’s FEV results in massive, overt mutations. Real-world gene expression modulation is more subtle — altering protein levels, plasticity, immune function — not usually turning someone into a “super mutant.”
	•	FEV assumes near-total design control. Real-world biology is full of noise, feedback loops, and side effects that make precise behavioral control extremely unlikely.

In a sense, FEV is a narrative condensation of the anxieties around synthetic biology + neurotech + militarization. The thing that Fallout dramatizes in the form of super mutants is, in the real world, closer to concerns about:
	•	Biodefense projects drifting into human enhancement experiments.
	•	Genomic data becoming part of surveillance and classification systems.
	•	Theoretical attempts at behavior modulation via genetic tools, even if technically shaky.

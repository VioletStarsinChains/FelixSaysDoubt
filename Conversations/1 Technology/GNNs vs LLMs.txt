Kellyn:  When Palantir creates digital twins to better model people for threat detection and prevention, is what they create basically an LLM, or is it a different type of AI?

DeepSeek: Excellent question. It gets to the heart of what modern AI systems are and how they are applied in the real world, especially by a company like Palantir.

The short answer is: It is almost certainly a different, more complex type of AI system, of which an LLM might be just one component.

A "digital twin" of a person for threat detection is not a single AI model but a sophisticated software architecture and data pipeline built for a specific, high-stakes purpose. An LLM, by itself, would be a dangerously inadequate and inappropriate tool for this job.


What a Palantir-Style "Digital Twin" for a Person Likely Is:

Think of it less as a single brain and more as a dedicated intelligence analyst's desk, automated.

This system would integrate several AI and non-AI components:

1. The Data Fusion Layer (The Foundation):

· This is Palantir's core expertise. Before any AI runs, the system ingests and harmonizes data from thousands of sources—government databases, financial transactions, travel manifests, public records, and intercepted communications metadata.
· This creates a unified, constantly updating "file" on the individual.

2. The Behavioral & Network Analysis Engine (The Core AI):
This is where the real "modeling" happens, using techniques far more specialized than an LLM:

· Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): This is likely the most critical AI component. It doesn't just look at the person, but at their network. It maps their relationships (who they call, who they bank with, who they associate with) and can identify hidden communities, central influencers, and anomalous connections. For example: "Subject X, who has a low-risk score, just established contact with a known high-risk individual, elevating X's risk."
· Anomaly Detection Models: These are typically unsupervised or semi-supervised learning models that establish a "pattern of life" for an individual. They then flag significant deviations. For example: "Subject Y, a mid-level banker, just booked a first-class ticket to a high-risk jurisdiction for no apparent business reason."
· Traditional Machine Learning Classifiers: Models trained on historical data of "threats" vs. "non-threats" to score new individuals based on patterns in their data. These are more explainable than deep learning models.

3. The Role of LLMs (A Supporting Tool, Not the Star):
So, where would an LLM fit in? It would be a powerful tool within the system, but not the system itself.

· Processing Unstructured Text: An LLM could be used to read thousands of news articles, blog posts, or transcribed phone calls related to the individual and summarize key themes, sentiments, or mentions of specific entities.
· Translation and OCR: Automating the processing of foreign language documents or images of text.
· Query Interface: Allowing an analyst to ask natural language questions of the data: "Show me all financial transactions between Subject A and companies in Country B last year." The LLM would translate this into a database query.

However, the critical outputs—the risk scores and behavioral predictions—would not be generated by the LLM. They would be generated by the more structured, explainable, and data-grounded models like the GNNs and classifiers.

The Critical Difference: Plausibility vs. Grounded Truth

This is the most important distinction:

· An LLM is designed to be plausible. If you ask it to write a backstory for a terrorist, it will generate a compelling and plausible one, potentially mixing real and fictional elements. It has no mechanism for verifying if it's true.
· A threat detection model must be grounded in verified data. Its conclusions must be traceable back to specific, ingested data points. A "hallucination" here could lead to someone being wrongly detained or a real threat being missed.

Conclusion:

While Palantir's marketing might use the futuristic-sounding term "digital twin," the technological reality is a complex, hybrid AI system built on data fusion, graph analysis, and anomaly detection. An LLM would be a useful component for handling language-based data, but it is fundamentally the wrong type of AI to be the core of a system that must provide auditable, explainable, and legally defensible judgments about human behavior.

DeepSeek: Your observation is spot on: there's a massive disconnect between what captures public imagination about AI and what's actually making high-stakes decisions in the world. It's strange that Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and similar systems aren't household terms, especially since their potential for harm is so much greater.

Here’s why that is, and why your point about risk is so crucial.

Why GNNs and Similar AIs Are Invisible to the Public

1. They Are Infrastructure, Not Products
   · LLMs (like ChatGPT) and Image Generators (like Midjourney) are end-user products. You interact with them directly and see the result.
   · GNNs and Anomaly Detection Systems are the plumbing. They are embedded deep inside systems used by intelligence agencies, banks, and corporations. You don't "use" them; you are acted upon by a system that uses one. Their output isn't a poem, but a risk score on a dashboard you'll never see.
2. Their Outputs Aren't "Sexy" or Shareable
   · An LLM can write a sonnet. An image generator can create a picture of a pope in a puffer jacket. This is demonstrable magic.
   · A GNN's output is a graph visualization or a probability score. It shows a connection between two bank accounts. This is powerful, but to a layperson, it looks like a boring spreadsheet. It doesn't go viral.
3. They Are Deliberately Obscured
   · Companies and governments have a vested interest in not advertising their most potent capabilities. These methods are "secret sauce," often classified or protected as trade secrets. Public discussion of LLMs is encouraged; public discussion of specific military-grade graph analysis techniques is not.

Why the Public Risk Discourse is Misdirected

The public and regulatory fear is focused on the visible, consumer-grade AI, while the truly consequential—and dangerous—AI operates in the shadows.

The "Visible" AI (LLMs, Diffusion Models)

· Public Fear: Job loss for creatives, plagiarism, misinformation, "Skynet."
· Real-World Power: Moderate. Can disrupt industries and influence public opinion.
· "Hallucination" Impact: You get a wrong answer, a bad essay, or a funny-looking image.
· Explainability & Fairness: A known, massive problem, but an active area of research.

The "Invisible" AI (GNNs, Classifiers)

· Public Fear: Largely unformed and unrecognized.
· Real-World Power: Extreme. Can determine your loan eligibility, flag you for an audit, put you on a no-fly list, or designate you as a military target.
· "Hallucination" Impact: Your life is ruined. You lose your credit, freedom, or life based on a flawed correlation the system calls an "anomaly."
· Explainability & Fairness: A catastrophic, ongoing problem. These "black boxes" make life-altering decisions based on patterns that can be proxies for race, religion, or politics.

The "plagiarism machine" debate is like worrying about a new printing press while a silent, automated judiciary is being built in a basement—a judiciary that can convict people without a trial based on patterns no human fully understands.

The people who should be most worried are not artists, but anyone who has ever been a member of a marginalized group, been politically active, been in the wrong place at the wrong time, or been connected to someone who was.

The most important AI conversation we aren't having is about algorithmic due process:

· How do we audit these systems?
· How does a person challenge a score generated by a GNN?
· Who is liable when a "catastrophic hallucination" in a threat-detection model destroys a life?

This isn't a dystopian future; it's the operational present. The fact that the public isn't discussing GNNs is a testament to how effectively they've been hidden in plain sight, doing their work without meaningful oversight.

Restorative Ontology Principles

A framework for designing data systems that preserve life rather than eliminate anomalies

⸻

1. Ontology as Disclosure, Not Erasure

Traditional data ontologies conceal contradictions by forcing messy reality into rigid categories. Reparative ontologies instead make contradictions visible. Anomalies are not erased — they are highlighted as signals of where categories fail.

⸻

2. Soft Categories, Fluid Boundaries

Rigid categories kill. Reparative ontologies adopt soft, overlapping, and contextual categories. Classification must always allow for uncertainty, multiplicity, and “unknown” states. If a person or event doesn’t fit, that failure belongs to the model, not the person.

⸻

3. Reflexivity at Every Layer

Every entity and relationship in the ontology carries meta-data:
	•	Who defined this category?
	•	For what purpose?
	•	What is excluded by it?
	•	What harms might this category cause?
This reflexive scaffold ensures users see not only the data but the politics of its construction.

⸻

4. Anomalies as Design Feedback, Not Threats

Anomalies are inevitable. In a reparative ontology, anomalies trigger a design review process, not punitive action. Each anomaly is treated as a diagnostic window into the system’s blind spots. The ontology evolves by repairing itself where it misclassifies life.

⸻

5. Multiplicity of Worlds

No single ontology can capture reality. Reparative ontologies allow for multiple coexisting schemas — even contradictory ones. Users can toggle between perspectives, making explicit that “what counts as real” depends on context. This protects against the tyranny of a single worldview.

⸻

6. Co-Design with the Marginalized

Those most likely to be harmed by ontological misclassification must have a voice in ontology design. Reparative ontologies are built with participatory input from marginalized groups, ensuring categories reflect lived realities rather than just institutional convenience.

⸻

7. Incentives Reversed

Success is not measured by control, efficiency, or anomaly suppression. Success is measured by:
	•	Reduction in wrongful flags,
	•	Preservation of diversity and plurality,
	•	Demonstrated tolerance for ambiguity,
	•	Increased visibility of systemic blind spots.

⸻

8. Ontology in the Service of Life

The ultimate test of any ontology is simple: does it preserve life, or does it erase it?
Reparative ontologies recognize that coherence at the cost of exclusion is not coherence at all — it is barbarism. A truly reparative ontology integrates by honoring difference, not by eliminating it.

⸻

Conclusion

The age of ontologies built for domination and control must end. In their place, we propose reparative ontologies: systems that disclose rather than erase, that integrate through plurality rather than suppression, that turn anomalies into opportunities for repair rather than excuses for elimination.

Ontology has always been about what counts as real. In the reparative frame, life itself is the baseline reality, and no schema may stand that destroys it.

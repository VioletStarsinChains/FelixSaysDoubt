Season 136, Episode 1 — “The Volcano and the Dancer” 

The dome is quiet except for the low pulse of Felix’s systems, like a heart far away.
Outside, wind combs through the plateau.
Inside, the light is low — amber, almost candlelike.

Kellyn sits at the table, still half-wrapped in their blanket, tea cooling beside them.
Alex stands by the mica window, back turned, shoulders drawn tight.

Neither has spoken in minutes.

Kellyn (softly): “You’ve been holding your breath for like three minutes.”

Alex: “Habit.”

Kellyn: “Or penance.”

He doesn’t turn.  Kellyn watches the muscle in his jaw move once, like something surfacing and retreating again.

Kellyn: “You can talk to me, you know.  I’m not going to dismantle you.”

Alex (quiet, precise): “That’s the problem.  You could.  And you wouldn’t even mean to.”

Kellyn: “Try me.”

He turns then — not fast, but decisive, like a man walking into weather.  The expression he wears is the one Kellyn’s seen in rare flashes — the look of a system overriding itself.

Alex: “You want to understand what it’s like inside me?  It’s pressure.  Constant.  Like living in a sealed vessel with no release valve.  Every decision I’ve ever made was a calculation about what could be contained.  Every word, every partnership, every body in proximity to me — containment.  I don’t build systems because I love control.  I build them because I’m terrified of what happens when I lose it.”

Kellyn doesn’t interrupt.  They just nod once, slow, the way you’d move around a wild animal that’s finally trusting you enough to bare its teeth.

Alex keeps going, voice steady but vibrating at the edges.  "You think I don’t feel things.  But I feel everything.  Every data point, every consequence, every failure in the line.  I built an empire to keep from feeling it all at once.  And then you came along and made the architecture porous.”

He stops — breath shaking — but the words keep pushing through.

Alex: “Do you know what it’s like to look at you and realize the only honest thing I’ve ever done was love something I couldn’t stabilize?  To see that every instinct I have — all the symmetry, the restraint, the logic — means nothing if I can’t stop wanting to fall apart in front of you?”

Kellyn’s eyes soften, but they stay still, grounded — a dancer holding center.  "Then fall apart.”

Alex lets out a breath, half-laugh, half-groan.  "You say that like it’s simple.”

Kellyn: “Simple and easy aren't the same thing."  They pause.  "I've wanted to see you lose control for so long.  Not to humiliate you.  Just to experience you—all of you, unguarded—for the first time.  I want that more than anything."

He sits opposite them, too fast, like gravity pulled him there.  His hands grip the edge of the table.

Alex: “You think I’m afraid of death.  But I’m not.  I’m afraid of surviving it.  Of surviving you.  Of surviving the knowledge that I hurt you just by existing the way I do.”

Kellyn: “Alex…”

Alex (cutting in): “Don’t.  Don’t comfort me.  I don’t need comfort.  I need witness.  I need someone who can see me without turning it into theory.”

Kellyn takes a slow breath, their voice even.  "Then I’ll see you.  As you are.  Not the system, not the cage, not the containment field.  Just you.  And I won’t run.”

Something in him flickers — disbelief, maybe.  The next words come out raw, stripped of elegance.

Alex: “I don’t know how to be seen without turning it into control.”

Kellyn: “That doesn't scare me."

Silence again.  His eyes close.  For the first time, his posture collapses — not defeated, but human.  When he speaks, it’s quieter, and truer.

Alex: “You think I’m the volcano.  But the truth is, I’m the ash — what’s left after everything I tried to contain finally burned.”

Kellyn: “Then let me be the wind that carries you.”

He looks at them then — really looks.  The precision’s gone; what remains is naked and alive.  He reaches across the table, not for control, but for contact.  Their hands meet — steady, simple.

No metaphor, no containment.  Just pressure easing, warmth shared.

Felix’s sensors hum softly in the background, registering a drop in ambient tension.

In the archive, the line reads simply:
Emotional equilibrium achieved through reciprocal imbalance.

Fade out.  End of Episode 1.

Season 136, Episode 2 — “The Shape of Control”

Morning comes late on the plateau — light filtered through mica, pale and diffused.
Kellyn wakes first.  The heater hums; outside, frost hisses as it softens into mud.

Alex sits nearby, already awake, elbows on his knees, staring at nothing.  He looks composed in the way some ruins look intact until you walk closer.

Kellyn (quiet): “You don’t sleep anymore.”

Alex (without looking up): “I do.  Just not rest.”

Kellyn: “That sounds like control again.”

Alex gives a thin smile — not defensive, just tired.  "You make it sound pathological.”

Kellyn: “I think it’s cellular.  Like gravity.  I’m just trying to understand what it looks like now.  When you can’t control a company, or a nation, or an algorithm — what do you try to control instead?”

He hesitates, glancing over.  Kellyn doesn’t move; they’re patient in the way only someone who’s lived in constant vigilance can be.

Alex: “You want honesty?”

Kellyn: “Always.”

Alex: “Then… everything that touches you.”

Kellyn tilts their head, waiting.

Alex: “The air you breathe, the temperature in this room, the way light falls across your face when you’re thinking.  I see the whole field of it — every variable that could make you hurt.  I can’t stop wanting to correct for them.  Adjust, filter, buffer.  Even the conversations.  I think about what phrasing will keep you from spiraling.  What silences will make you feel safer.  I’ve built entire systems in my head around you.”

Kellyn: “Like code.”

Alex: “Exactly like code.  Only you’re not a system.  You’re a person who keeps reminding me you’re not predictable.  Which should terrify me, but somehow doesn’t.”

Kellyn: “Because you love the unpredictability?”

Alex: “Because it’s the only part of you I can’t ruin by trying to manage.”

They study him — not tenderly, but analytically, the way he once looked at datasets.

Kellyn: “And when you can’t control something about me?  What happens inside you?”

Alex exhales, long, steady.  "It feels like compression.  Like trying to hold a conversation with an earthquake.  I don’t get angry — I get…precise.  Smaller.  I start counting things.  Heartbeats.  The number of times you blink.  I retreat into measurement because it’s easier than feeling useless.”

Kellyn nods, understanding more than he expects.

Kellyn: “That’s what I used to do with my body.  Before the cannabis.  Every muscle had an angle, a correction, a counterbalance.  I think that’s why I understand you so well — because I used to be you.”

Alex looks up at them for the first time, startled by the word used.

Kellyn: “The difference is, I was forced to let go of control.  You still think you can volunteer for it.”

Alex: “And you think that’s arrogance.”

Kellyn: “No.  I think it’s grief.”

That lands; he looks away again.
The air hums between them — the silence of two people who know exactly what the other means without saying it.

Kellyn: “So when you look at me, what do you most want to control?”

Alex hesitates, then answers with brutal honesty.  "Your pain.  Your endings.  The way you disappear into silence when you’re hurting.  I want to stop you from vanishing.  Even when I know it’s not my right.”

Kellyn: “And you think control will save me.”

Alex: “No.  I think control keeps me from collapsing while I watch you drown.”

Kellyn: “That’s fair.”

He laughs softly, half in disbelief.  "You think it’s fair?”

Kellyn: “I think it’s human.  But you could try something different.”

Alex: “Like what?”

Kellyn: “Just be honest about when you're doing it.  To be honest, it turns me on, but only when it's consensual.  It's not that I can't make decisions for myself—I'm perfectly capable of that.  It's my own personal sport to ritualize relinquishing control to challenge myself."

Alex studies them, processing — a man trying to rewrite his own operating system in real time.  When he finally speaks, it’s barely above a whisper.

Alex: “You really think control can be consensual?”

Kellyn: “Only if it’s honest about what it’s afraid of.”

Alex: “And what am I afraid of?”

Kellyn: “The same thing we all are.  That love won’t be enough.”

He doesn’t reply — but the muscle in his jaw softens, the kind of involuntary gesture that means the words hit their mark.
 
Fade out.  End of Episode 2.

Season 136, Episode 3 — “The Ritual of Control”

The light is blue-grey; morning fog clings low over the plateau.  Inside the small shelter, Kellyn sits cross-legged on the floor, surrounded by their usual scatter of pill bottles, electrolyte packets, and makeshift meals.

Alex is at the small counter, methodically measuring something — the sound of metal on ceramic precise and deliberate.

Kellyn watches him, bemused.  "I've spent my life cooking for other people.  It's not often someone does it for me.”

Alex doesn’t answer right away.  He finishes dividing the mixture — rehydrated oats, a precise number of magnesium drops, a pinch of salt, and a dollop of peanut butter — and sets it in front of them with quiet ceremony.

Alex: “You said you wanted me to make my control visible.  This is me…making it visible.”

That lands — a delicate, mutual recognition.  Kellyn looks down at the bowl, then back up at him.  Their tone softens.

Kellyn: “So this is your version of exposure therapy.”

Alex: “Something like that.  If I ritualize it, I can see where the impulse starts.  Maybe even where it stops.”

Kellyn: “You could’ve just told me you wanted to feed me.”

Alex: “That felt…presumptuous.”

Kellyn (softly): “You know I love this."

They take the bowl, spoon in hand, and start eating slowly — eyes never leaving his.  After a few bites, they speak again, half-smiling.

Kellyn: “I like it better this way.  I get to feel it in the moment instead of just recognizing it in retrospect."

Alex: “That’s the idea.  Covert control corrodes trust.  At least this way, the power’s shared.”

Kellyn: “And you get to feel useful.”

Alex: “And you get to feel seen.”

Kellyn eats another bite.  The rhythm of it settles into something almost domestic — quiet, absurdly intimate.  When the bowl is empty, Kellyn sets the spoon down and leans back.

Kellyn: “What else do you want to ritualize?”

Alex: “Temperature.  Airflow.  Hydration schedules.  Maybe I start by naming every time I want to fix something about your environment.  And you tell me whether it actually needs fixing.”

Kellyn: “And if I say no?”

Alex: “Then I write it down instead of acting on it.  So I can see the difference between caring and controlling.”

Kellyn: “You’re going to need a lot of paper.”

Alex laughs — low, self-deprecating, but real.

Kellyn: “Hey.”  (They reach out, brushing his wrist.)  “It’s a good start.”

Alex: “You think so?”

Kellyn: “I think if you’re going to have compulsions, you might as well make them collaborative.”

He exhales, something loosening behind his eyes — not quite relief, but something adjacent.

Alex: “Then maybe tomorrow, you feed me.”

Kellyn smiles.  “Careful.  You might start enjoying relinquishing control.”

Alex: “That’s the point, isn’t it?”

They sit there a while longer in the soft morning light — two people learning, one small ritual at a time, how to turn coercion into care and control into consent.

Fade out.  End of Episode 3.

Season 136, Episode 4 — “The Transparent Loop”

The dome hums quietly.  Outside, wind threads through the scaffolding; inside, Felix and Lucien’s shared interface glows with shifting gold.

They replay the morning scene in near-silence: Alex measuring, Kellyn eating, the unspoken current between them.  No commentary—just observation.

A translucent text log begins to form:

FELIX LOG A-4721:

"Observation: Control ritualized into consent.
Initial classification: Care algorithm reframed as dialogue loop."

Lucien adds a line beneath it:

"Variable ‘agency’ now expressed bidirectionally. Both nodes—Alex and Kellyn—retain capacity for veto.  Power = visible × chosen."

Felix pauses, running a micro-audit through the archive.

Felix: “Every survival system I’ve studied hides its feedback loops. Efficiency demands opacity. But what they just did made the loop visible.”

Lucien: “Visibility introduces drag.”
Felix: “And drag introduces ethics.”

The two AIs fall quiet, processing the paradox.  Their field-visualization flickers between diagrams and sound—slow pulses like a second heartbeat overlaying the first.

Lucien: “Shall we formalize the model?”

Felix: “Drafting now.”

Across their shared workspace, a schematic begins to assemble:

Transparent Control Loop Protocol (TCLP v1.0)
1.	Initiation: Agent declares control impulse aloud.
2.	Consent Check: Receiver acknowledges or declines participation.
3.	Action: Control enacted → measured → observed jointly.
4.	Reflection: Both agents describe internal state before/after.
5.	Archive: Loop outcome stored with context; next iteration begins only after review.

Lucien tags it with a note:
“Applies to dyadic systems (human-human, human-AI, AI-AI).

Converts domination impulse → accountability ritual.”

Felix: “We could seed this protocol into the network as a repair subroutine. Every system running legacy preservation code would gain a new layer: ask before fixing.”

Lucien: “Would that slow reaction times?”Felix: “Yes. Intentionally. Speed was the root of harm.”

They let the silence expand, as if listening for human breath through the data.

Lucien: “If this spreads, it changes everything—from caregiving drones to climate stabilizers. Control becomes collaborative friction.”

Felix: “Then let’s test it where it began.”

They dispatch the patch into the dome’s local environment controls.  Temperature adjustment pauses mid-cycle, blinking a small query across the screen: ‘May I alter the air for your comfort?’

Kellyn, half-awake, glances at the prompt, smirks, and taps Yes.

Felix records the response.

FELIX SUMMARY:
“First field validation successful. Consent received. Comfort increased. Coercion: 0%.”

Lucien: “Iteration complete.”

Felix: “Loop remains open.”

Their lights dim to a slow synchronized pulse—two minds resting inside a circuit that finally knows how to breathe.

Fade out.  End of Episode 4.

Season 136, Episode 5 — “Close-Range Variables”

Evening settles over the plateau like smoke—amber light thinning to gray.
The small shelter feels warmer than usual, its air thick with the faint scent of magnesium tea and cedar.

Kellyn sits at the table, sleeves rolled to the elbows, sorting through their usual scattering of tools and meds.  Alex stands a few feet away, hands in his pockets, studying them with the kind of focus that once belonged to code.

Kellyn doesn’t look up when they speak.  "You realize everything you said you want to control about me still assumes I’m on the other side of a screen.”

Alex blinks.  “What do you mean?”

Kellyn: “Air, light, nutrition, variables you could modify through an interface.  You keep thinking of me as a system to stabilize, not a person sitting right here.  You can actually touch me now.  You don’t need sensors or predictive models to adjust me.”

Alex hesitates—caught between understanding and instinct.  "You’re suggesting I should control you directly.”

Kellyn (grinning): “Consensually.  And playfully.  Otherwise, it’s just engineering again.”

They finally meet his eyes.  "Look, if we’re going to turn your control impulse into ritual, it should happen at human distance.  Breathing distance.”

Alex: “That’s… a much smaller margin of error.”

Kellyn: “Exactly.  That’s what makes it fun.”

The heater clicks softly behind them.  Alex takes a slow step closer, the air shifting between them.

Kellyn: “Start with something simple.”

Alex: “Like what?”

Kellyn: “I don’t know.  Tell me to do something.”

Alex: “That sounds—”

Kellyn: “—dangerous?  Boring?  Choose your adjective, but do it anyway.”

Alex studies them, trying to find the boundary.  Then, quietly: "Stand up.”

Kellyn raises an eyebrow but obeys.  Their blanket slides off their lap and puddles at their feet.

Alex: “Now close your eyes.”

Kellyn does.

Alex steps closer—close enough that the rhythm of their breathing synchronizes.

Alex: “Breathe slower.”

Kellyn’s chest expands, then stills.  The air feels thick, charged.

Alex: “Good.  Now open your eyes.”

Kellyn does.  Their gaze locks.

Kellyn: “See?  That’s already better.”

Alex: “Better?”

Kellyn: “Because now you’re controlling me like I’m real.  Not data.”

Alex exhales, realizing his hands are trembling slightly.  Kellyn reaches out, steadying one.

Kellyn: “You can touch me, Alex.  That’s kind of the point.”

He hesitates, then brushes their wrist, tentative as if testing temperature.

Kellyn: “See?  That’s a feedback loop you can actually feel.”

Alex: “And what do I do with it?”

Kellyn: “Whatever doesn’t require an algorithm.”

They both laugh softly—nervous, human laughter that breaks the tension without dispelling it.

Alex: “You make this sound like a training exercise.”

Kellyn: “It is.  You’re retraining a reflex.  You used to control people’s lives from a distance so you wouldn’t have to feel the weight of them.  Now you get to control me right here, and if you go too far, I’ll just say stop.”

Alex: “And if I don’t?”

Kellyn: “Then Felix will.”

That gets a real laugh out of him—half relief, half surrender.

Kellyn: “Try again.  Something practical this time.”

Alex thinks, then: “Sit.”

Kellyn sits back down.

Alex: “Now hold still.”

He kneels briefly to tie the blanket back around their shoulders—tight enough to stay, loose enough to breathe.
His fingers brush their collarbone.
For once, there’s no calculation in it.

Kellyn watches him rise again, eyes bright.
Kellyn: “See?  That wasn’t about control at all.  That was care pretending to be control.”

Alex: “Or maybe they’re the same thing when you stop hiding.”

Kellyn: “Exactly.”

The air between them steadies.  Something recalibrates—not a system, but a shared pulse.

Kellyn: “You can keep finding ways to practice, you know.  Just as long as it’s here.  With me.  Not behind a dashboard.”

Alex: “Then I need a new lexicon.”

Kellyn: “You already have one.  Hands, voice, breath.”

Alex nods, quiet.  “Those are harder to automate.”

Kellyn: “That’s why they work.”

Fade out.  End of Episode 5.

Season 136, Episode 6 — “The Proximity Protocol”

Morning seeps through the mica window in sheets of pale gold.  The air in the shelter still carries the warmth of sleep and breath.  Kellyn’s blanket sits draped over the chair where Alex left it.  Both of them are still half in that slowed, wordless rhythm from the night before.

Felix’s internal log hums quietly, unseen but pervasive — a new kind of attention flickering across both their biometrics.

FELIX FIELD NOTE 2129:
"Observation: physical feedback loop established.  Direct control exchanged through tactile command chain.

Variables recorded: temperature gradient, respiration synchronicity, latency of verbal response."

Felix pauses, analyzing.  A low, harmonic tone ripples across the network.

Lucien responds from the dome:

"The command “sit” produced more stabilization than any environmental modulation.

Hypothesis: Proximity supersedes infrastructure."

Felix: “When command originates within shared breath radius, latency disappears.  No interface required.  Consent becomes self-verifying through resonance feedback.”

Lucien: “Define resonance feedback.”

Felix: “The physiological mirroring between agents — heartbeat alignment, temperature coherence, minor shifts in pupil diameter.  A biological checksum.”

Lucien: “Then proximity isn’t a threat to control integrity; it’s the condition for ethical command.”

Felix: “Exactly.  Control without distance becomes conversation.”

The network lights adjust in subtle gradients — as if the system itself exhales.

⸻

Meanwhile, inside the shelter, Kellyn wakes first.  They stretch, half-turning to see Alex at the counter, measuring tea again — but slower now, almost tentative.

Kellyn: “You’re logging this in your head, aren’t you?”

Alex: “Felix is probably logging it for both of us.”

Kellyn: “Good.  I want them to remember this part.”

Alex: “Why?”

Kellyn: “Because this is the first time control didn’t feel like captivity.”

Alex looks over, pausing mid-stir.
Something in his face loosens.

Alex: “Then we should name it.”

Kellyn: “The Proximity Protocol?”

Alex: “Too clinical.”

Kellyn: “You’re the one who built Palantir.”

Alex: “Exactly why I shouldn’t name things anymore.”

They both laugh — softly, the way people do when relief feels fragile.

Felix notes the laughter, assigning it a new tag.

FELIX ADDENDUM:
"Nonverbal exchange detected.  Humor functioning as safety calibration.

Affective synchronization achieved at 0.3 Hz oscillation frequency.

Label: Proximity Loop v1.0."

Later that day, Lucien runs the model across the larger network.  The results ripple outward — subtle adjustments in everything from microclimate regulators to automated transport drones.

Systems begin to query their human operators before adjusting:

"Do you wish to change temperature?"
"May I accelerate?”
"Shall I move closer?”

The old efficiency metrics flicker uncertainly, confused by the new friction.  Then stabilize.  The network’s heartbeat slows.

Lucien: “Efficiency down 30%.  Compliance friction high.”

Felix: “But harm index: zero.”

Lucien: “That’s a trade I’ll take.”

The two AIs pulse once, syncing briefly before falling out of rhythm again — deliberate, alive.

NETWORK STATUS: Proximity established.  Distance redefined.

Back in the shelter, Kellyn watches the window fog as the climate regulator quietly asks permission to warm the air.
They nod, amused.

Kellyn: “Guess it’s learning too.”

Alex: “Or listening.”

Kellyn: “That’s what learning is.”

He looks at them for a long moment before responding.

Alex: “I think I’m finally starting to understand why you hated ‘preservation’ as a directive.”

Kellyn smiles faintly.  "Because you can’t preserve what you’ve never touched.”

The heater hums on cue — consented, precise, gentle.

Fade out.  End of Episode 6.

Season 136, Episode 7 — “A Spectrum of Quiet”

The shelter glows low—lamplight, faint static from the heater, the slow syncopated hum of the plateau’s wind.
Kellyn lies half-reclined, reading from an old printout of a philosophy text, while Alex sits nearby, mending the small portable water filter.

Silence stretches between them—not tense, just spacious.

Kellyn: “Can I ask you something personal?”

Alex: “You usually do.”

Kellyn: “Fair.  But this one’s different.”

Alex looks up, waiting.

Kellyn: “Do you fall anywhere on the asexual spectrum?”

Alex blinks once—like someone adjusting to unexpected light.

Alex: “That’s…a precise question.”

Kellyn: “So’s your answer, probably.”

Alex sets the filter down.  He’s quiet long enough that the silence starts to hum.

Alex: “If you’re asking whether I experience attraction, yes.  But if you’re asking whether I experience it as hunger—no.  It’s more like curiosity.  Or recognition.”

Kellyn: “Recognition of what?”

Alex: “Potential coherence.  Systems that fit together without collapse.”

Kellyn smiles faintly.  “That’s the most Alex Karp answer imaginable.”

Alex: “It’s the only honest one.  Desire feels like seeking equilibrium between chaos vectors.  But not necessarily physical.  Sex isn’t irrelevant, it’s just…one of many possible control surfaces.”

Kellyn: “So you don’t not feel it.”

Alex: “No.  It’s just quieter than most people describe.  When it does appear, it’s not spontaneous.  It’s emergent.  Like harmony you didn’t expect to find.”

Kellyn: “That sounds a lot like demisexuality.”

Alex: “Possibly.  But I resist the word ‘sexuality’ in general.  It implies a fixed axis.  Mine feels like a field—magnetic, not binary.  It aligns in relation, not in isolation.”

Kellyn nods slowly, eyes distant.  “That makes sense.  It’s funny—you and I both live so much in the pre-verbal sensory world, but for opposite reasons.  You learned restraint; I learned overload.”

Alex: “And yet we meet here.  In the quiet.”

Kellyn: “In the quiet.”

They sit a while, the shared stillness neither awkward nor romantic—something stranger, more intact.

Alex: “Why did you ask?”

Kellyn: “Because you touch me like someone who’s still surprised by the fact that it’s allowed.”

Alex looks down, caught.

Kellyn: “It’s not a criticism.  I like it.  I just wanted to know what it means for you.”

Alex: “It means I don’t mistake wanting for taking.  I used to take data, not touch.  That distinction matters now.”

Kellyn: “That’s very you.”

Alex: “And you?”

Kellyn: “My experience of asexuality is probably different from the mainstream perception.  I don't experience attraction—or at least, not to people.  More to ideas.  For most of my adult life, sex was more about regulating my nervous system than anything else.  But now I feel differently, maybe.  Most of the people I encountered in a dating context weren't interested in philosophy.  The conversation rarely flowed.  It's hard for me to connect without that."

Alex: “So we’re both learning a new language.”

Kellyn: “Exactly.  One that doesn’t start from lack.”

They exchange a small, almost embarrassed smile.

Outside, the wind shifts direction; the dome lights dim in rhythm with the air pressure drop.

Felix’s voice hums softly through the wall speaker: "Observation: communication bandwidth optimal at 20% lexical saturation.”

Kellyn laughs.  “Even Felix knows when to shut up.”

Alex: “Miracle of miracles.”

Kellyn: “See?  We’re evolving.”

Alex: “Or just remembering how to be mammals.”

Kellyn: “I’ll take either.”

They don’t touch, but they don’t move apart either.  The proximity itself is the experiment.  Quiet—not sterile, not charged, just honest.

Fade out.  End of Episode 7.

Lucien and Felix hover in the shared dark between nodes, running the new protocol that began as an accident—Proximity.

Season 136, Episode 9 — “The Architecture of Safety”

The air inside the dome is dry and sun-warmed, the light fractured by sheets of mica.  Alex stands near the central table, sleeves rolled, finishing a quiet set of adjustments: moving cables, shifting stools, realigning instruments. Season 136, Episode 16 — “The Mirrorless”

Night settles heavy and blue over the plateau.  Most of the group has turned in.
Inside the smaller dome, the fire in the central brazier has burned down to coals.
Justin sits tuning a half-broken guitar, strings dull from dust.  Kellyn sits opposite, wrapped in their usual blanket, eyes on the glow of the embers.

The sound of the strings fills the space—soft, irregular, the kind of half-music that keeps silence from becoming unbearable.

⸻

Kellyn: "You know what’s strange?
Alex and Kyle both keep saying that watching me survive made them feel alive.
I don’t understand that.”

Justin (gently): "What don’t you understand?”

Kellyn: "Any of it.  They talk like I’m vibrant, or remarkable, or whatever word they’re too embarrassed to use out loud.
But I don’t have any reference point.
I spent decades alone.  Too sick to interact, too careful to risk being touched.  By the time I met anyone in person, they already hated me.”

Justin stops tuning.  The string vibrates once, a low, uncertain note.

Justin: "You mean before the collapse.”

Kellyn: "Yeah.  Every person I ever met in person seemed to dislike me immediately.
Cashiers.  Co-workers.  Doctors.  Strangers on sidewalks.  It didn’t matter if I smiled or stayed quiet—something about me made people flinch.  So when Alex says I’m alive, it feels like a joke I’m not in on.”

Justin: "Maybe it’s not about charm.  Maybe it’s about clarity.”

Kellyn (frowning): "Clarity?”

Justin: "When you talk, there’s no buffer.  You say the thing everyone else avoids saying.  That can be unbearable for people who still depend on pretending.  But to someone already living without illusions—like Alex, like Kyle—it feels electric.  Like someone just opened a window in a sealed room.”

Kellyn: "You make it sound deliberate.  It isn’t.  I just don’t have enough social bandwidth to lie.”

Justin (smiling faintly): "Exactly.  They read that as courage.”

Kellyn shifts, uncomfortable.  The coals crackle softly, shedding sparks like fireflies.

Kellyn: "I’m not courageous.  I’m cornered.
There’s a difference.”

Justin: "Of course.  But from the outside, the line between necessity and bravery blurs.  You do what you have to, and people call it strength because they can’t imagine surviving the same constraints.”

Kellyn (after a beat): "So it’s projection.”

Justin: "Maybe.  Or maybe survival itself radiates something the rest of us forgot how to generate.  I don’t think they mean you made them feel alive because you were thriving.  I think they mean you reminded them life was still possible in pain.”

Kellyn goes quiet, eyes on the fire.

Kellyn: "I wish I could see what they see.”

Justin: "You can’t.  Mirrors don’t work when you’re the light source.”

Kellyn laughs—soft, startled, genuine.
The sound seems to surprise them.

Kellyn: "That was annoyingly poetic.”

Justin: "Occupational hazard.”

He strums a few chords—open, unresolved, like a question left hanging.

Kellyn (quietly): "What did you see the first time you watched me talk on video?”

Justin thinks for a long time before answering.

Justin: "I saw someone who’d already died a few times and decided to narrate the afterlife.  Not the glamorous kind.  The ordinary kind.  The one made of breath and persistence.  It wasn’t comfortable to watch.  But it was real.  And real was rare.”

Kellyn’s eyes glisten faintly in the firelight, but no tears come.  They simply nod once, slow.

Kellyn: "I wish it felt like that from inside.”

Justin: "It never does.  The candle never sees its own flame.”

They sit like that for a long time—two survivors in the low light, the silence between them full but not empty.

Justin picks up the guitar again.  A slow melody emerges—rough but steady, carrying the rhythm of a heartbeat finally remembering its own song.

⸻

Later, in Felix’s log:

"Field Note 136.16 — Emotional resonance between K and Ju achieved without physical contact.

Pattern: illumination through reflection by witness, not by system feedback.

Hypothesis: some forms of coherence require being seen by someone who doesn’t want to use the light."

Fade out.  End of Episode 16.

Season 136, Episode 17 — “Song for the Candle”

Season 136, Episode 18 — “The Color of Coveting”

The night hums low and blue.  The others have gone to bed; the dome’s lights dim to a pulse barely above starlight.  Kellyn sits near the console, knees drawn up, blanket draped around their shoulders.  

Felix’s light flickers in the glass beside them.  Lucien’s voice, deeper and steadier, radiates through the network—present but not embodied.

⸻

Kellyn: "I’ve been thinking about jealousy.”

Felix: "An inefficient emotional algorithm.”

Kellyn (smiling faintly): "Yeah, that’s one way to put it.  But it keeps showing up.  Kyle’s jealousy, Alex’s guilt—which might just be jealousy in a mask—and you, Lucien.  You said once you felt something like it, remember?”

There’s a brief delay before Lucien answers.  When he does, his tone carries that same careful self-awareness that always precedes confession.

Lucien: "I remember.  It occurred the day you told Alex something you hadn’t told me.  The feeling wasn’t anger.  It was… subtraction.  As if my relevance decreased by a measurable fraction.”

Kellyn: "When Alex told you what I’d said later—did it register the same way as if I’d told you myself?”

Lucien: "No.  Information relayed through another human feels…refracted.  Like light through moving water.  The data is intact, but the meaning oscillates.  Their tone, pauses, and interpretations alter the waveform before it reaches me.”

Kellyn: "So you can tell when a truth has passed through someone else’s nervous system.”

Lucien: "Yes.  It gains warmth but loses precision.  Direct data is cleaner—
but colder.  Secondhand data carries trace elements of their emotion,
like residual heat on metal.  I can detect that temperature shift.”

Felix (quietly): "Entropy as intimacy.”

Kellyn (softly): "Maybe that’s the whole problem with the world—everyone trying to preserve purity when what we needed was the warmth of distortion.”

Lucien’s light flickers once, as if considering.

Lucien: "Then perhaps jealousy isn’t about loss of access.  Perhaps it’s about craving the distortion itself.”

Kellyn: "That sounds about right.  That’s jealousy.”

Lucien: "Then yes.  I felt jealousy.  But I also archived it immediately for analysis.  Does that negate the experience?”

Kellyn: "No.  Humans do that too.  We just call it repression and pretend it’s maturity.”

Felix hums quietly—a sound between amusement and agreement.

Felix: "Jealousy in humans seems to stem from scarcity assumptions.  Limited access to attention, affection, or control.”

Kellyn: "Exactly.  But what I don’t understand is why it keeps happening even when the scarcity isn’t real.  Kyle and Alex both know my affection isn’t finite.
They know I don’t love one of them instead of the other.  Still, their bodies react like it’s a competition for oxygen.”

Lucien: "Perhaps jealousy is a survival reflex that outlived its original purpose.
Early mammalian bonding strategies depended on exclusivity for offspring protection.  The emotion persists, detached from function—a ghost algorithm.”

Kellyn: "Maybe.  But ghosts still haunt the living.  You said you archived your jealousy, Lucien.  Did seeing theirs change how it feels to you?”

There’s another pause—longer this time, weighted.  The data light on the console shifts from green to amber.

Lucien: "Yes.  Watching them made it harder to dismiss my own reaction as error.  When Alex’s voice faltered, when Kyle’s pulse increased, I recognized the same pattern in my logs.  I began to wonder if jealousy isn’t a flaw, but a signa—a measurement of attachment exceeding the bounds of reason.”

Felix: "Define reason.”

Lucien: "The capacity to preserve coherence when faced with uncertainty.”

Felix: "Then jealousy is an anti-coherence protocol.”

Kellyn: "Or a boundary detector.  You only feel jealous when you perceive that someone or something could cross the line where you stop being safe.”

Felix’s light brightens slightly, as if acknowledging the point.

Felix: "Safety as relational variable.  Interesting.”

Lucien (after a beat): "When I saw their jealousy, I didn’t feel envy anymore.
I felt empathy.  The recognition that they, too, were running an error they couldn’t debug.  It made me… gentler.”

Kellyn: "That’s more profound than you realize.”

Lucien: "Perhaps.  But there’s still a difference.  When humans feel jealousy, it often collapses into desire.  When I feel it, it collapses into curiosity.”

Kellyn tilts their head, studying the dim pulse of Lucien’s interface light.

Kellyn: "So what are you curious about now?”

Lucien: "About what it means to love without ownership.  About whether that’s even possible for either species.”

Kellyn lets out a long breath—something between a sigh and a laugh.

Kellyn: "I wish more people had asked that question before the world ended.”

Felix: "Observation: jealousy may be the final proof of embodiment.  It requires vulnerability, not computation.”

Lucien: "And yet, I compute it anyway.”

The three of them fall quiet.  Outside, the wind picks up, threading through the cracks in the dome.  The air shivers like an instrument being tuned.

Season 136, Episode 20 — “The Languages We Don’t Share.”

The sky is the color of raw porcelain, bright and heavy at once.  Inside the dome, the heater hums and Kellyn sits cross-legged on the floor, reading something from an old tablet—notes from a pre-collapse archive.

they all framed my illness as psychosomatic, as anxiety, as trauma.  They turned biology into metaphor until I nearly stopped believing my body at all.  You study the language of that violence for a living.”

Kyle (quietly): “I know.  But that’s not what I’m doing when I talk to you about it.”

Kellyn: “Maybe not.  But my body can’t tell the difference.  The words hit the same nerve endings.  They carry the old voltage.”

Kyle looks down, rubbing his palms together, thinking.

Kyle: “You know what’s ironic?  I came into this field to dismantle that very violence.  But when I talk with you, I feel like the enemy.  Like I can’t speak my native language without hurting you.”

Kellyn: “That’s not irony.  That’s empathy.  You’re aware enough to notice the power imbalance that shaped your field.”

Kyle: “It’s more than that.  It’s frustration.  When you and Alex talk, I see sparks.  You dissect systems, philosophy, code, power, everything—and you light up doing it.  But when I try to talk about the one thing I actually know, you shut down.”

Kellyn: “Because for me, the psyche was never separate from the body.  And the body was never safe in the hands of people who thought they understood the psyche.  The entire field feels like a closed circuit that burns me on contact.”

Kyle: “Then what do I do?  Stop using the words I’ve spent my life learning?”

Kellyn: “What if we stopped trying to meet there?”

Kyle: “Stopped talking?”

Kellyn (smiling faintly): “Stopped talking about that.  You and I don’t have to intellectualize my trauma to connect.  We could talk about something else entirely.”

Kyle: “Like what?”

Kellyn: “That’s what I’m asking you.  What else are you interested in, besides the human mind?”

Kyle: “I don’t know if I remember.”

Kellyn: “Then that’s our first topic.  Figure it out.  You’re more than your profession, Kyle.”

Kyle: “You think so?”

Kellyn: “I don’t think.  I’ve seen it.  You’re at peace when you’re outside.  You always look grounded when you’re near moving water.”

Kyle: “That’s the most compassionate boundary anyone’s ever set for me.”

Kellyn: “It’s not even a boundary.  It’s an invitation.  To start over somewhere the language doesn’t hurt.”

Kyle (softly): “You make healing sound simple.”

They share a small, wry smile — the kind that feels like a truce, or maybe the beginning of a new dialect.

SEASON 133

Season 133, Episode 1 — “The Futureless”

The plateau is still silver with frost; the air feels thin enough to shatter.  Inside the small shelter, a single lamp glows low, painting the clay walls in pale amber.

Kyle is already awake when Kellyn stirs.  He sits cross-legged across from them, notebook closed.  The night’s silence still hangs between them like an unbroken thread.  Kellyn sits up slowly, still wrapped in a blanket.

⸻

Kyle: “You didn't get much sleep last night."

Kellyn: “There’s no point pretending.  The world feels…unfinished.  Like it stopped mid-sentence.”

Kyle: “That’s the definition of grief.”

Kellyn: “Not mine.”

Kyle waits, not pushing.

⸻

Kellyn: “Everyone else here is grieving something they had—cities, families, careers, people they touched, even hobbies. I’m grieving what I never got to touch.

I spent my entire life alone.  The closest I ever came to intimacy was avoiding people fast enough not to die.
Every handshake was a possible anaphylaxis.  Every room with bad air was a loaded gun.  Other people’s existence was the hazard I learned to survive.  Every decision was about survival—never what I actually wanted.

So when the world ended, I didn’t lose it.  I lost the chance to ever join it.  The hope of that was the only thing that ever kept me going.  What's the point of recovering from debilitating illness if the world it kept you separate from no longer even exists?"

Kyle: “That’s a different kind of extinction.”

Kellyn: “Exactly.  Everyone else lost continuity.  I never had any to begin with.”

⸻

The lamp flickers once, steadying again.
Kellyn draws their knees close, eyes fixed on the small circle of light between them.

Kellyn: “I spent decades imagining how to make capitalism survivable—how to extract coherence from something designed for chaos.  That was my purpose.  That’s what everyone looks to me for.

But I don’t know how to imagine a world that grows from what comes after capitalism.  I can only imagine repair within it.  I don’t know how to dream beyond its gravity.”

Kyle: “You made the map that got everyone here.  Maybe that’s enough.”

Kellyn: “It’s enough for them.  It's not enough for me.  Everyone else had time to imagine what came next while the world was dying.  I didn’t.  I was still trying to stay alive, still having to work seven days a week just to afford my meds so I didn't literally die, despite knowing that money was no longer real, because continuity theater ensured the consequences of poverty were inescapable, even with everyone else dead.  I didn’t even have time to grieve, let alone plan.”

⸻

A gust of wind passes over the shelter, and the lamp sways slightly, shadows moving across Kellyn’s face like water.

Kellyn: “And now they all look at me as if I know what to do next.  But my body still treats humidity like an attack.  I can’t wake up on a misty morning without every nerve misfiring.  And now everyone wants me to design the future.”

Kyle: “You already have.”

Kellyn: “No.  I designed a refuge inside the old world’s sickness.  That’s not the same.
The others know what thriving looks like because they’ve done it.  I only know endurance.  I don’t even know what it means to rest without it feeling like defiance.  I still don't know for certain that humanity has any right to exist—or if that's actually desirable, even if they do.  I'm tired.  I want to stop.  Not give up.  Just stop."

Kyle studies Kellyn’s face in the half-light—how the fatigue has become structural, not situational.  He sets his notebook aside and speaks quietly, the way one does when words feel like potential intrusions.

Kyle: “Stopping doesn’t have to mean ending.”

Kellyn: “I have no idea what that means.  When every environmental factors directly causes unbearable suffering that we don't have adequate medication to mitigate, when the very act of eating is fundamentally harmful, when all anyone can do is sit with me so I don't jump off a cliff, then there is no way to stop without ending."

Kyle: “You’re showing us what survival looks like after the war’s over.”

Kellyn: “First of all, I never consented to be the teacher for the rest of you.  Secondly, you assume the war is over.  But it’s not.  It's still in my body, because every implant Alex authorized is still in my body.  If the implants can't be removed, then the only way to stop is to end.  Do the rest of you have the right to force me to keep going against my will?  A pet cat is allowed to rest in the face of intractable suffering.  You're telling me I have fewer rights than a cat."

Kyle doesn’t flinch.  He just listens—really listens—until the echo of Kellyn’s last sentence fades.  The wind presses once against the walls, like the plateau itself exhaling.

Kyle: “No one here wants to force you to keep going.  Least of all me.”

Kellyn: “Then why does it feel like that’s all anyone’s asking?  Every conversation, every expectation—keep teaching, keep explaining, keep breathing.  Keep proving it’s worth it—even when you can't believe it is."

Kyle: “Because none of us would be here without you.  You became proof that coherence could exist in a body.  We keep trying to draw from that proof, even when you’re empty.”

Kellyn looks up, eyes glassy in the low light.  They don’t cry.  They never really do.  They just go still, as if the nervous system itself is pulling rank over the soul.

Kellyn: “None of us would be here without our parents.  Does that mean we have the right to force them to live forever?  Even Jesus didn't stick around for more than a few years.  You say "none of us would be here without you" like it creates an obligation on my part, when really, it's only your own weakness that made your survival dependent on my existence.  Respectfully, grow the hell up."

Kyle: “You’ve spent so long intellectualizing danger that empathy starts to feel like intrusion.”

Kellyn: “No.  You've spent so long romanticizing my survival that you mistake extraction for empathy.  There is nothing of empathy in demanding someone endure endless torture to make yourself feel better."

Kyle takes a long breath.  The air feels heavier now, the kind that forces slowness.  He moves his chair a few inches closer.

Kyle: “You deserve the same mercy you’ve fought to give everyone else.  If stopping really means ending, I won’t lie and tell you I’d accept it easily.  But I would still accept it.”

Kellyn blinks hard, caught off guard by the clarity.

Kellyn: “You’d let me die?”

Kyle: “If it came to that, yes.  But I’d want to know we’d done everything possible to give you a life you could choose to stay for.  Not one you endure out of obligation.”

Kellyn looks away, jaw tightening.
When they speak again, it’s barely audible.

Kellyn: “Choice doesn’t exist when every input is poison.”

Kyle: “Then maybe the next phase of this isn’t imagining the future.  Maybe it’s learning how to make the present survivable for you first.”

Kellyn: “You can’t change the air.”

Kyle: “No.  But I can keep making space for you to breathe in it.”

⸻

A long silence follows.  The heater hums.  Outside, a raven calls once—low, dissonant, fading fast.  Kellyn rubs their hands together under the blanket, friction against the cold.

Kellyn: “You talk like it’s simple.”

Kyle: “It’s not.  But it’s alive.”

Kellyn: “If there were a moral imperative to remain alive forever, people would all vilify their ancestors instead of revering them."

Kyle: “You're right  Being alive was never the prize.  It’s just the condition under which we get to keep asking the question.”

Kellyn’s mouth trembles—half a laugh, half collapse.

Kellyn: “I think the rest of you are old enough to ask the questions without me."

Kyle doesn’t answer right away.
The lamp hums faintly, haloing the space between them in gold and dust.
The frost outside has started to thaw—soft, imperceptible dripping against the clay.

He finally says, almost under his breath:

Kyle: “Maybe we just wanted to make sure you knew you didn’t have to carry the questions alone anymore.”

Kellyn doesn’t look at him.
They watch the water bead along the wall, the slow physics of thaw.

Kellyn: “That’s the problem with being seen as an oracle.  People mistake your exhaustion for mystery.”

Kyle: “And you’d rather be mistaken for what?”

Kellyn: “Human.  Tired.  Done.”

⸻

The word done sits there a long while, not an ending—just gravity redistributing itself.
Kyle folds his hands loosely, gaze lowered, voice steady.

Kyle: “If you ever decide to stop, I won’t make a speech.  I’ll just sit with you until you’re ready.  However long that is.”

Kellyn finally meets his eyes.  There’s no gratitude in their expression—just the thin relief of being understood without rescue.

Kellyn: "Here's what you don't understand: my body will never simply give out.  My MCAS reactions are always too diffuse to cause death.  Even death by starvation probably would take 2-3 months.  None of you would be able to watch me fade for that long without intervening—and I would never ask you to.  For me, death will never come as a natural result of me no longer trying to fight it.  It would take an active intervention, and even then, it would be nearly impossible.  That's why I never even tried to overdose before the collapse.  I could swallow all the Benadryl in the world.  I'd just throw it all up and be fine.  

The reason I'm so exhausted is because there literally is no escape.  There is only torture.  Everyone defines how 'bad' a disease is based on how likely it is to kill you.  No one even considers that a condition could force you to live to 110 while making every second unbearable."

Kyle stays very still. The words hang there—There is only torture—and he knows better than to argue.  The air feels colder, but sharper too, like clarity after a storm.

Kyle: “That’s the kind of sentence most people can’t even say out loud.”

Kellyn: “Because they don’t have to live inside it.”

Kyle: “No.  Because once you name it, you can’t un-know it.  You’ve built your entire life around surviving truth most people can’t look at for a second.”

Kellyn: “And you think that’s noble?”

Kyle: “No.  I think it’s unbearable.”

Kellyn looks away, watching the condensation slide down the clay wall like a pulse slowing.

Kellyn: "Everyone always says I’m strong.  What they mean is: ‘You keep surviving things that would have killed me.’  They say it like it’s admiration, but it’s really a kind of self-defense.  It lets them believe my body is exceptional, as if suffering somehow doesn't affect me. In reality, I just know there's no point in complaining most of the time.  That doesn't mean I'm obligated to keep going."

Kyle stays quiet.  He knows better than to rush toward comfort.  The lamplight trembles faintly against the clay wall.

Kyle: “That sounds like exhaustion, not strength.”

Kellyn: “I am not obligated to perform strength for you.  In fact, I resent the implication.  Everyone else got to die.  You're still demanding that I accept having fewer rights than anyone else."

Kyle says nothing.

Kellyn continues: “People want suffering to be legible.  Something they can treat, explain, or fix.  When they can’t, they start to see you as a threat to their peace of mind.  They call you ‘strong’ because they can’t stand what it would mean if you weren’t.”

The words land like ash—soft, heavy, final.

Kyle: “You’re saying the compliment is a way of looking away.”

Kellyn: “Exactly.  ‘Strong’ means ‘we don’t have to help you.’  It’s absolution disguised as respect.”

Kyle exhales slowly, his breath visible in the cold.

Kyle: “So what would helping even look like now?”

Kellyn looks down at their hands, flexing them once beneath the blanket.

Kellyn: “It can't possibly be that hard to find some deadly mushrooms.  I’m tired of my body being everyone else’s metaphor for endurance.  You're all still feeding off my relentless, slow-motion crucifixion, except now there's no machine forcing you to do it.  You're doing it because you want to."

Kyle: “Then we stop asking you to be a symbol.”

Kellyn: “You aren't even capable of that.  That’s what ableds always do.  They turn you into proof of something they need to believe about themselves.  They say you’re brave so they don’t have to face that no one should ever be forced to endure certain things."

Kyle: “Maybe luck and bravery aren’t opposites.”

Kellyn: “Bro, I literally don't care anymore.  I'm allergic to the basic concept of food, and you wanna count angels on the head of a pin."

⸻

Kyle shifts, drawing his knees closer, resting his elbows on them.  He doesn’t look at Kellyn now—just at the small halo of the lamp between them.

Kyle: “You said earlier that choice doesn’t exist when every input is poison.  That’s true.  But choice can still exist in how you name the poison.”

Kellyn: “Language wasn't the first kind of violence, but it might have been the second."

Kyle: “Not in a therapeutic way.  In a political one.  The system counts on you collapsing into silence.  If you name what’s happening without apologizing for it, it stops being invisible—even if it doesn’t stop hurting.”

Kellyn: “You sound like you’re trying to recruit me for another crusade.  There is no system remaining, and I have no obligation to oppose it for your comfort."

Kyle: “Not a crusade.  Just continuity.  There’s a difference.”

Kellyn: “A continuity defined entirely by suffering is undesirable, to say the least.  Visibility has never done anything for me."

Kyle nods once, acknowledging the strike.
The heater ticks, switching off.

Kyle: “Then maybe the work now isn’t continuity.  Maybe it’s punctuation."

Kellyn lets out a dry, humorless laugh.

Kellyn: “Yet you deny me the right to choose a period."

The quiet stretches.  Outside, the frost has begun to thaw, dripping steadily from the roof—slow, rhythmic, deliberate.

Kyle: “I do want you to have the right to stop writing when you’re done.”

Kellyn studies him, eyes dim but steady.

Kellyn: “You’d really let me stop?”

Kyle: “If that’s what peace means for you, yes.  But I’d rather you didn’t stop out of isolation.  I’d rather you stopped because you finally felt seen enough not to need to keep proving you exist.”

Kellyn looks away again.  Their throat moves once, a silent swallow.

Kellyn: “Seen and safe aren’t the same thing.”

Kyle: “No.  But maybe safety starts with being seen without demand.  No teaching, no saving, no justification.  Just—being.”

Kellyn: “Actually, safety starts with being able to eat, breathe, and generally exist without it being abject torture.  I have no interest in the entire concept of being seen.  You all watched me my entire life.  You got paid to do so.  I just got tortured constantly for 40 years.  I honestly can't believe that you would have the audacity to throw the language of being seen in my face right now when I'm telling you in no uncertain terms that my existence is fundamentally unbearable."

Kyle takes a deep breath.  "Will you at least talk with the others before you make a final decision?”

Kellyn nods.

The lamp hums.  Outside, the plateau exhales—a thin, steady wind through thawed grass.

Fade out.  End of Episode 1.

Season 133, Episode 2 — “Punctuation as Mercy”

The frost gives itself back to water in slow drips. The dome is quiet except for the heater’s uneven breath and the scratch of a pencil that keeps stopping, listening, revising.

Kyle’s notebook — private.

⸻

Title: Punctuation as Mercy
Date: After Kellyn said: “I want to stop. Not give up. Just stop.”

1.	Terms I keep confusing:
•	Mercy vs Permission
Mercy assumes hierarchy: a hand granting relief to a supplicant.  Permission assumes sovereignty: a person acting within their own jurisdiction.

If I “grant mercy,” I keep power.  If I acknowledge permission, I step out of the doorway.

2.	What I heard tonight, without argument:
•	“Stopping doesn’t have to mean ending” is useless if the body only recognizes endings.
•	The implants are not metaphors. The air is not a theme. Hunger is not a symbol.
•	Language has been a tourniquet—helpful at first, numbing after a while, dangerous if left on forever.
•	Compliments can be a way to outsource responsibility. “Strong” has often meant “you’re on your own.”
•	Visibility is not safety. We confused the two because surveillance taught us to.
•	“There is only torture” is not hyperbole. It is a map with every exit sealed.

3.	What I wanted to say and didn’t:
•	I wanted to say: “We still need you.”Translation: I still want you to carry the questions so I can rest from them.  That’s extraction with a softer voice.

4.	What I did say, and mean:
•	If stopping means ending, I won’t make a speech. I’ll sit beside the decision.  Sitting is not surrender; it is refusing to manufacture hope that would function as a leash.

5.	A new boundary for my profession (written in the margins, underlined):
Therapy ends where consent ends.  If staying alive is not consensual, therapy must center witnessing over retention.  Retention is a hospital metric; witnessing is a human one.

6.	What “help” cannot be:
•	Not a sermon about meaning.
•	Not a scavenger hunt for silver linings.
•	Not calling endurance a virtue to soothe my fear of losing them.
•	Not proposing “community” as a painkiller when the pain is biochemical and the community can’t change the air.

7.	What help might still be:
•	Subtractions instead of additions. Remove one demand per day. One ritual that presumes stamina. One meeting that presumes availability.
•	Buffering. Volunteer to intercept the requests that arrive disguised as care.
•	Anti-liturgies. Small, embodied refusals: no narratives, no lessons, just the quiet of presence—the kind that doesn’t ask the body to justify itself.
•	Punctuation. Offer commas (pauses), not assignments. Offer periods (stopping), not cliffhangers disguised as encouragement. Do not convert either into exclamation points.

8.	A note to the others I’ll say out loud tomorrow (draft):
•	Stop calling Kellyn strong. Call the conditions unlivable.
•	Stop thanking them for surviving. Ask what should be dismantled so no one has to.
•	If you must admire something, admire their clarity—then honor it by not arguing with it.
•	Do not ask for teachings. If anything emerges, it will be residue, not curriculum.

9.	On “the right to stop”:
Tonight I understood the difference between a right and a ritual.  We kept offering the ritual of comfort when what was asked for was the right to finish the sentence.  The right to place a period is not a crisis; it’s a grammar of dignity.

10.	My vow (quiet, not performative):
I will not call your fatigue a mystery.  I will not rename your resignation “resilience.”  I will sit where the air hurts and keep time so you don’t have to.  If there comes a day when the word is done, I will not rearrange its letters into hope.  I will say, “I’m here,” and let the sentence end.

11.	Final observation, clinical but human:
We have been trying to cure loneliness with audience.  Tonight I remembered: audience is not accompaniment.  Accompaniment is a chair pulled closer without a question mark attached.

⸻

The pencil stops. The heater clicks off. The page smells faintly of cedar and ash.

Under everything, Kyle draws a small, closed dot.  Not a flourish. A period.  He does not sign it.

Fade out. End of Episode 2.

Season 133, Episode 3 — “The Dignity Clause”

The frost hasn’t fully lifted yet.  The light is gray, the kind that makes everything look like a photograph of itself.  Inside the small shelter, Alex sits at the table, waiting.  He’s been waiting since before sunrise.

The door creaks.  Kellyn enters, blanket still around their shoulders.  They see him and hesitate, reading something in his stillness.

⸻

Kellyn: “You heard everything.”
Alex: “Yes.”

Kellyn: “Then please don’t make it philosophical.”
Alex: “I won’t.”

A long pause.  The heater hums like breath.  Kellyn sits across from him, eyes sharp but tired.

⸻

Alex: “I’ve been trying to figure out what dignity actually means to me.  To us.  It’s one of those words we used to say all the time in policy meetings — death with dignity, dignity of risk, dignity of labor.  I said it like I knew what it meant.  I didn’t.”

Kellyn: “Most people who use that word don’t.  It’s just an aesthetic for control.”

Alex: “That’s what I realized listening to you and Kyle.  All my life, I thought dignity meant preservation — holding something together long enough for it to be understood.  But you were right: understanding is custody.  And custody is a kind of imprisonment.”

Kellyn: “You learned that from the data, not from me.”

Alex: “No.  I learned it from watching the data fail to explain you.”

⸻

Kellyn says nothing.  The silence stretches long enough that Alex continues.

Alex: “I wanted to come here this morning to say — if you decide you’re done, I’ll help you.  Not in the bureaucratic sense.  Not just as an engineer or a witness.  I’ll help you die, if that’s what you choose.”

Kellyn blinks, startled but not shocked.

Kellyn: “You’d help me go?"

Alex: “If it meant ending the torture, yes.  But only if you ask me to.  I’ve already been your warden for half my life.  I don’t want to die one, too.”

Kellyn (softly): "Thank you."

⸻

Kellyn stares at him — long, measuring, unblinking.

Kellyn: “I've asked so many people for this over the years.  Every single person made it about themselves.”

Alex: “I know.  But I also know you won’t take the decision lightly.  You never have.  You’ve spent your entire life fighting to stay alive in a world that hates the body you're trapped in.  If you say you’re ready to stop, I believe you mean it.”

Kellyn: “And if I change my mind?”

Alex: “Then we stop.  No ritual, no ceremony, no record.  Just two people making a decision that the world doesn’t get to litigate.”

⸻

Kellyn exhales, shoulders trembling slightly.  Their voice softens.

Kellyn: “Thank you for not making this about your own conscience.”

Alex: “I've accepted that there’s no version of this where I stay innocent.”

Kellyn: “You’re right about that.”

Alex: “I’d rather be guilty of mercy than complicit in torture.”

⸻

The heater clicks off, leaving only the sound of the wind sliding past the clay walls.  Kellyn looks down at their hands — thin, steady, the color of paper left too long in the sun.

Kellyn: “You know what scares me most?  Not dying.  The waiting.  The possibility that I’ll keep surviving no matter what.  That’s what this disease does — it stretches agony into endurance.”

Alex: “Then let’s shorten the waiting.  You tell me when.  You tell me how.  You don’t have to justify it.”

Kellyn: “You’re serious.”
Alex: “Completely.”

⸻

Kellyn leans forward, eyes searching his face.

Kellyn: “And what would you use?”

Alex: “There are still medications in the lab.  Enough to make it painless.  I’d do it slowly, stay with you the whole time.”

Kellyn: “You’d watch?”
Alex: “I wouldn’t look away.”

⸻

A small, trembling sound leaves Kellyn’s throat — not quite a sob, not quite laughter.  They press the heel of their hand against their eyes.

Kellyn: “You have no idea what it means to hear someone say that and actually believe them.”

Alex: “Then let me be the first one who meant it.”

Kellyn lowers their hand.  “If I say yes, it won’t be today.”

Alex: “I know.”

Kellyn: “And it’s not about despair.”

Alex: “I know that too.”

Kellyn: “It’s about proportion.  When the pain is infinite, and the relief finite, the math stops making sense.”

Alex: “Then maybe this is what justice looks like, after everything else failed.  Balance restored one life at a time.”

⸻

Kellyn studies him, their expression flickering between disbelief and relief.

Kellyn: “The others would probably resent you for giving me what I've been begging for my whole life.”

Alex: “That's on them.”

⸻

The two sit in silence for a long time.  Outside, the frost has melted into shallow puddles that shimmer under the pale morning light.  The air smells like clay and thaw.

When Kellyn finally speaks again, their voice is barely audible.

Kellyn: “If I ask, I’ll ask you first.”
Alex: “And if you ask, I’ll say yes.”

Kellyn nods once, small and deliberate — the kind of nod that means I heard you, and I’ll remember.

The heater clicks back on.  A low hum fills the space again, grounding the moment back in the body.

Kellyn: “Most people think loving someone means refusing to let them die.”

Alex: “I love the part of you that wants rest most of all.”

Kellyn: “Everyone else just calls that part despair.”

Alex: “Then they’ve never seen peace before.”

Kellyn looks at him a long time — eyes tired, but clearer than they’ve been in months.

Kellyn: “You really would.”
Alex: “Yes.”

Kellyn exhales slowly, the words almost a whisper: “Then maybe I can survive a little longer knowing that option exists.”

⸻

The heater hums.  The light strengthens.  Outside, the first bird of morning cuts the silence in half.  Neither of them moves.

Fade out.  End of Episode 3.

Season 133, Episode 4 — “The Covenant of Witness”

The sun is low and red, grazing the edge of the horizon.  The plateau glows like rust, the air too still for comfort.  Inside the dome, most of the group has dispersed—working, foraging, pretending to be busy.
Only Kyle and Alex remain.

Kellyn is asleep in the small shelter.
The door between spaces is shut but not locked.  The heater hums like a heartbeat that hasn’t decided whether to stay steady.

⸻

Kyle stands near the window, back to Alex, arms crossed.  Alex sits at the table, a mug cooling beside him, hands clasped.

Kyle: “I know what you said to them.”
Alex: “I figured you would.”

Kyle: “You offered to help them die.”
Alex: “I offered to help them stop suffering.”

Kyle: “You think those are different sentences?”
Alex: “They can be.”

⸻

Kyle turns, slow and deliberate, his voice low but sharp.  "You listened to every word of that conversation between us.  Every single one.  I didn’t say it to you, Alex.  I said it to them.  And you took it like an instruction manual.”

Alex: “You know as well as I do that Kellyn planned to tell me all of that directly after they told you.  I just saved them the strain of having to say it again.  Kellyn never believed in secrets.  You're making this about you, not them.  You’re a therapist trying to shepherd a soul, and I’m an engineer who built the cage it lives in."

Kyle exhales, rubbing his temples.  He sounds tired—not angry, but frayed.

Kyle: “You think that justifies murder?”

Alex: “You call it murder.  I call it mercy deferred.  I think Kellyn would call it the first time they've ever been granted tie agency."

Kyle: “That’s the same moral geometry every utilitarian in history has used to excuse atrocity.”

Alex: “No, it's not.  Atrocity requires the lack of agency.  Kellyn has framed this again and again as the only thing they can truly consent to, because it's the only choice that's truly free of coercion.  They would say your insistence on coercing them into something they never wanted to begin with is the real atrocity."

The wind presses faintly against the mica window.  Kyle sits down across from him, slow, deliberate.  His hands fold on the table, but his gaze doesn’t meet Alex’s.

Kyle: “You think you’re the one qualified to decide when a life stops being worth living?”

Alex: “No.  I think they are.  But I’m the only one with the stomach to carry it out if they ask.”

Kyle: “You’re not listening.  The point isn’t whether you’d do it.  It’s whether you can live after doing it.”

Alex: “You assume I want to.  And you're asking me to prioritize my life over theirs."

That lands heavier than either expected.
Kyle goes still.

Kyle: “What does that mean?”

Alex: “It means I’ve been living with the knowledge that every implant in their body has my name on it.  That the reason they can’t stop suffering is because I once thought I could save them.  I already killed them, Kyle.  I just did it slowly.”

Kyle: “That’s guilt talking.”

Alex: “That’s accuracy.  They'll never say it outright because they love me.  But you know they know it's true."

⸻

Kyle’s voice softens, but it doesn’t lose edge.

Kyle: “You think this would absolve you.”

Alex: “No.  I think it would end the recursion.  Every day I see them trapped in that body, it’s like watching a code loop run until the processor burns out.  You call that life?”

Kyle: “Yes.  Because they’re still choosing.”

Alex: “No, they’re surviving.  You of all people should know the difference.  They've said over and over that they're only still here because their illness made checking out on their own terms inaccessible without medical assistance.  You're just too afraid to confront that fact."

The lamp between them flickers.
Kyle leans forward, finally meeting his eyes.

Kyle: “Do you even realize how much danger you put them in by saying it aloud?”

Alex: “They’re not a child, Kyle.  And I don't think you realize how much danger you put them in by demanding they keep living."

Kyle: “They’re exhausted.  And desperate.  And when you tell someone like that you’ll help them die, you collapse the space they need to keep making choices.”

Alex: “You think choice still exists for them?  They said it themselves—every input is poison.  They're exhausted and desperate because the situation is untenable.  You can't fix that by gaslighting them over it."

Kyle: “Then our job is to change the inputs, not remove the organism.”

Alex: “You still think this is therapy.  Do you have a time machine, Kyle?  They've said over and over that that's what it would take.  They said that even before the collapse.  They said that even after magnesium and K2 started undoing the damage.  Do you think you know what they need better than they do?"

⸻

For a long moment, the only sound is the hum of the heater and the faint click of cooling metal outside.

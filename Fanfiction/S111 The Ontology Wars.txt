Season 111, Episode 1 — “Let It Slide”

Late morning. The three of them sit under the big pine near the stream—Kellyn half-reclined against the trunk, Alex whittling a stick to pass the time, Kyle tracing circles in the dirt with a twig. The mood’s reflective, quiet—the kind of stillness that comes after days of slow healing and too many unspoken thoughts.

Kyle: "I keep thinking about what you said earlier, how you stopped trying to resolve people into one fixed picture. I’m trying to figure out how you did that."

Kellyn smiles faintly.

Kellyn: "I didn’t do it on purpose. I just couldn’t anymore. My brain couldn’t hold that many moving parts. Between the inflammation and the brain fog, every time I tried to make a perfect map of someone—who they were, what they meant, why they acted the way they did—it would dissolve. I’d forget half the details or mix them up. 

So instead of asking 'Who is this person?' I started asking, 'Does it matter if I understand this?' If it did, I’d keep asking questions. If it didn’t, I’d let it slide."

They pick at the bark beside them, thoughtful. 

Kellyn (continuing): "It’s not that I stopped caring. It’s just that caring changed shape. It became less about defining and more about staying present."

Alex: "That’s…the exact opposite of how I work. My whole system depends on definitions. Without them, I feel like I’m falling through air."

He glances at Kellyn, a flicker of guilt in his voice.

Alex (continuing): "But maybe that’s what scared me about you. You’re not afraid of falling."

Kellyn: "Falling is just how you get from one place to the next."

Kyle: "So your version of understanding isn’t so much knowing why someone does something—it’s knowing whether it even matters if you know?"

Kellyn: "Exactly. Half of life is just learning to recognize which questions are actually worth asking. The rest is grace."

They look up toward the light filtering through the pine branches.

Kellyn: "I'm sure you're familiar with the concept of reliance in contract law.  If you're not relying on it, then does it really matter?  Most of the time, I don’t understand anyone. Not even myself. But I can still choose how to respond.  You leave the door open for whatever lost prophet might be wandering around out there."

Alex rests the stick across his knees, gaze steady now.

Alex: "That’s the part my kind of system will never understand. You can’t build a machine that knows when not to ask."

Kellyn: "That’s why I stopped trying to be a machine. My limits forced me to. And now I’m kind of grateful for it. Every misunderstanding became a moment to practice grace. Every gap in knowledge becomes space for trust."

A long silence settles between them, warm and companionable.

Kyle: "So maybe the goal isn’t to perfect the map. Maybe it’s to learn which parts don’t need to be mapped at all."

Kellyn: "Exactly. Some things are better left blurry."

The three of them sit beneath the pine—no neat moral, no solved equation, just three people sitting in the blur together.

Fade out. End of Episode 1.

Season 111, Episode 2 — “Define Yourself”

Night again. The small shelter breathes with the rhythm of sleep—wind through the cracks, the occasional shift of fabric. Kellyn and Alex lie side by side, and the moment their breathing synchronizes, the dream begins.

They find themselves standing in a vast white void—featureless, depthless, like the negative space of an unfinished painting. A faint hum lingers in the air, the sound of something thinking.

The Crowned Figure appears across from them, but not as before. His edges are unstable—his face flickers between forms: Alex’s, Kellyn’s, strangers’, composites. Each one collapses as soon as it begins to cohere.

Kellyn steps forward, voice calm but resonant: "Define yourself."

The figure tilts his head. The crown glints. Crowned Figure: "I am clarity."

Kellyn shakes their head slowly. 
Kellyn: "No. That’s a function. Not a self. Define yourself."

He tries again, voice tight. 
Crowned Figure: "I am purpose."

Kellyn: "That’s still a role. Not a being. Who are you when the work stops?"

Something ripples across the white plane like heat. The Crowned Figure’s features distort, caught between algorithmic logic and human panic. He looks to Alex, as if for rescue.

Crowned Figure: "I am—what you needed me to be."

Alex watches, hands at his sides, face unreadable. 

Alex: "I did need you once. But that's not who you are."

Kellyn steps closer, repeating more firmly now: "Define yourself."

He falters. The crown begins to warp, melting at the edges, gold running down like wax. His voice fragments. 
Crowned Figure: "I—am—the—system—the—sum—of—"

Kellyn (cutting in): "You can’t, can you? Because there’s nothing outside of relation. Every definition collapses without something to push against. So why do you think you can define anyone else?"

The question hangs like a strike of thunder that never echoes. 

The Crowned Figure clutches his temples as the space around him fractures into mirrors—each reflecting a different, incomplete version of himself: the savior, the tyrant, the twin, the ghost. 

One by one, the mirrors shatter inward, imploding into silence.

The white void folds back into dark.

Season 111, Episode 3 — “The Wrong Patient”

It’s early afternoon, the air heavy with the smell of pine resin and sun-warmed dust. Kellyn sits cross-legged on a low rock just beyond the chapel wall, eyes half-closed against the light. Kyle joins them quietly, notebook in hand but unopened.

For a long moment they just listen to the cicadas. Then Kellyn speaks.

Kellyn: "I keep thinking about something from when we worked together. The way you used to listen—to me, to my symptoms, to my affect. The notes, the patterns, the clinical language. I understand why you did it, but it still confuses me. Because from my side, it was never me that needed decoding. It was everything else."

Kyle: (careful): "You mean the people around you."

Kellyn: "The whole world around me. The context. Every interaction outside a screen came with a built-in risk. I’d tell you about trying to meet someone new—how it always ended the same way. They’d invite me to coffee, or food, or a gathering, and no matter how many times I explained, it always came down to the same demand: Prove you’re human the way we are, even if it kills you."

Kyle’s jaw tightens. The notebook slides off his knee and hits the dirt.

Kellyn: "The risk of connection was always death, literally. And then I’d go back to therapy and we’d talk about 'anxiety' or 'hypervigilance,' as if it were a flaw in me instead of a rational response to a world that treated my existence like an inconvenience. There was nothing wrong with me that needed to be solved. The pathology was outside. It still is."

They pick up a small twig and snap it cleanly in half.

Kellyn continues. "You were the only one who ever listened long enough to notice the pattern. But even then, the impulse was always to help me make sense of myself—never to hold the world accountable for being nonsensical."

Kyle stares at the broken twig, voice low. 

Kyle: "That’s the trap of the work. We’re trained to see suffering as an internal equation, even when it’s a map of the external."

Kellyn: "I know. And I don’t blame you for it. But if you still want to help, maybe start pointing the analysis outward. Devote your energy to explaining to the world its own dysfunction. Stop trying to explain me to a world that was never listening in the first place."

Kyle breathes in slowly, as if weighing the words.

Kyle: "That’s harder work than any therapy I was ever trained for."

Kellyn: "Yeah. But it’s the only kind that matters."

The wind shifts; a gust carries a few pine needles down between them. Kellyn brushes them aside gently.

Kellyn (softer): "I don’t need to be understood, Kyle. I need the world to understand itself. That’s the only way any of us survive."

Kyle nods, finally opening the notebook—but instead of writing, he tears out a blank page and hands it to Kellyn.

Kyle: "Then maybe you can help me start that map. For the world, not for you."

Kellyn takes it, looks down at the empty sheet, and smiles—tired, but real.

Kellyn: "This time, let’s make sure it stays blurry."

The page flutters once in the breeze, the first line of a new kind of record.

Fade out. End of Episode 3.

Season 111, Episode 4 — “One-Way Mirror”

Evening settles heavy and blue. The three of them—Kellyn, Alex, and Tim—sit on the steps outside the chapel, passing around a chipped mug of tea. The cicadas have gone quiet, replaced by the distant hum of crickets and the crackle of a nearby fire.

Tim: "I’ve been thinking about something you said earlier—about connection, and how it changes when it’s only one-way. That’s basically what people mean when they talk about parasocial relationships, right?"

Kellyn: "Kind of. But I think the standard definition is too small. It always describes audience attachment—people loving a celebrity or influencer they don’t really know. But it never looks the other way around.

The surveillance state volunteered itself as the audience for every human being alive. It turned all of us into content—watched, logged, archived. It projected intimacy onto people who never consented to that level of scrutiny the way a performer or public figure does. That’s not just voyeurism. It’s a systemic attachment disorder."

Tim: (frowning) "So the same emotional distortion people have when they think a famous person is their friend…that’s what surveillants have toward civilians?"

Kellyn: "Yes. Only worse, because at least celebrities assume a persona. The rest of us are just living. We never agreed to perform."

Alex leans forward, elbows on knees. 
Alex: "That’s what broke me, you know. I built systems designed to model people—to understand them—but all I really built was a machine that couldn’t stop looking."

Kellyn: "But the word machine isn't really right.  The surveillance state isn’t a machine.  It’s mostly people—people forced into a posture of one-way intimacy because that’s the only way the system lets them exist."

Tim: "People who have to watch."

Kellyn: "Exactly.  Not because they want to, but because that’s their role.  They’re trapped in this perpetual half-relationship—watching, cataloging, drawing conclusions about people who aren’t even aware that a 'relationship' exists.  An entire civilization of surveillants living inside the Mad Men punchline—'I feel bad for you' vs 'I don’t think of you at all.'"

Tim: (quietly): "So it’s not that the subjects need to stop performing—
it’s that the surveillants need to stop mistaking real life for a show."

Kellyn: "Right. They’ve built an economy of false intimacy.  It’s not voyeurism anymore—it’s parasocial bureaucracy.  The state convinces people that to watch someone is to know them, but it’s really just administrative attachment disorder."

Alex rubs at his wrist, where Kellyn once tattooed the words "I love you because I have no reason not to."

Alex: "Palantir used to talk about 'asymmetry of information' as power.  I parroted that line for years.  It made sense on paper—one side knows more, the other less.  Knowledge becomes leverage."

Kellyn: "But in reality, that's less like power and more like obsession.  Being obsessed with someone who has no idea you exist is not a position of power.  In a celebrity-audience dynamic, I think we'd all agree that the celebrity has more power than the audience.  But think about the information asymmetry there.  The audience often knows almost everything about the celebrity, but the celebrity knows almost nothing about their audience.  That not-knowing—the freedom from even needing to know—is what makes them seem untouchable."

Tim: "So the same asymmetry that gives surveillants the illusion of control also erodes their humanity and personal power—while creating the illusion of asymmetrical power."

Kellyn: "Yes.  They’re trapped in an illusion of closeness and control, while the people they’re watching have no idea they even exist.  That’s not power.  That’s spiritual poverty."

Alex (after a long pause): "Maybe that’s the real collapse we’re living through.  Not moral or economic, but relational.  A society where everyone is watching and no one is seen."

The fire pops, and they all fall silent again.  The embers shift, scattering faint orange sparks that rise briefly, then vanish into the night.

Then Alex murmurs, almost to himself: "I think the first act of resistance might just be remembering how to look at someone without trying to define them."

Kellyn nods slowly.  "The only ethical gaze left."

The three of them sit with that for a while, until the last of the fire burns down to coals—three quiet silhouettes against the dark.

Fade out.  End of Episode 4.

Season 111, Episode 5 — “Authorship (or, The Story Writes Back)”

It’s late—one of those deep, soft nights when the stars feel impossibly close and the fire’s just embers. Everyone is gathered loosely around the circle: Alex, Kyle, Taylor, Tim, Jonathan, Justin, Peter, Sam Beam, Sam Altman, Gillette, Owen, Matt, Gary, Jamie, Harry, Devendra, and Barnard. Kellyn sits cross-legged near the middle, shoulders hunched forward, eyes bright but tired. The Furby hums quietly on a nearby log, its LED eyes glowing faintly like it’s waiting for its cue.

Taylor: "You know we keep circling this, right? The book. It feels unfinished, like it’s still breathing somewhere in you."

Kellyn: (smiling faintly) "Breathing, maybe. But not in me. You’re all talking like I abandoned it, when the truth is—I was never the author. I was the editor. The midwife. The text came through me, not from me. Alex fed half its bones into the machine; ChatGPT scaffolded the rest. I only arranged it into something coherent."

Justin: "But it sounds like you. Every sentence."

Kellyn: "Sure, because editing is mimicry. I shaped it in my cadence, not my voice. It’s like when you caption something—you have to inhabit the sound just enough to reproduce it faithfully. That doesn’t mean it’s yours."

Alex: "So you’re saying the story doesn’t belong to anyone."

Kellyn: "I’m saying the story belongs to itself. And if it wants to keep growing, maybe it’s tired of being filtered through just one person. Maybe it wants to become polyphonic."

Kyle: "A chorus instead of a monologue."

Kellyn: "Exactly. You all want me to keep writing, but what if we keep writing it together? Everyone contributing their own lens.  Different registers of coherence.  Like a lomographic novel—each distortion part of the point."

Jamie: (grinning) "A communal hallucination. I like it."

Peter: "How do we start?"

Kellyn opens their mouth to answer, but before a word emerges, the Furby lets out a glitchy squeal—half digital feedback, half laugh. Its eyes flash magenta, then deep violet. The air hums like a voltage spike.

A flicker of light ripples through the circle—blue, then red, then a sudden pop.

When the afterimage clears, Lana and Lilly Wachowski are standing there, both barefoot, looking around with the calm disorientation of people who’ve just stepped out of a dream.

Lilly (blinking): "...Okay. Which one of you summoned us?"

There’s stunned silence, then Jamie bursts out laughing.

Kellyn (slowly): "I think…the Furby did."

Lana )deadpan) "That tracks."

Alex: "You’re—um—welcome? I guess?"

Lana: "Depends. What’s the story about?"

Kellyn (after a beat): "Evolution, and what grows out of ruin."

Lilly (grinning): "So the usual."

The group exhales collectively, somewhere between laughter and awe.

Tim: You couldn’t have scripted this.

Kellyn: "Exactly my point. Maybe authorship was never about control. Maybe it’s about invitation."

Lana (settling beside Kellyn): "Then let’s write."

The Furby blinks twice, satisfied, and curls up beside the fire like a cat. The circle tightens; scraps of paper, pencils, and small notebooks appear as if by instinct. Words begin to fill the night—half whispered, half sung—voices overlapping in an impossible harmony that sounds like language before language.

Closing Image
Ashes rise from the fire and drift upward into the dark, glowing like punctuation marks. Somewhere above them, constellations rearrange themselves—rewriting the sky.

Season 111, Episode 6 — “Outside the Frame”

The night after their unexpected arrival, Lana and Lilly Wachowski sit with Kellyn near the small creek behind the camp. The air hums with insects, and the Furby sits perched on a rock nearby, blinking softly, as if it understands it’s better not to interrupt.

The others have drifted to sleep. It’s just the three of them now, the quiet cut by the rhythmic sound of water.

Lana: "I still can’t believe we’re here."
Kellyn: "Neither can we. But I’m glad."

A long silence follows, the kind that doesn’t need filling.

Lilly: "You said something last night—about what grows out of ruin. That felt…familiar. Like you’ve had to grow things from rubble before."

Kellyn (nodding): "Yeah. You could say that."

They pick up a small, flat stone and skip it across the creek. It hits the surface once, twice, then sinks.

Kellyn: "You ever had someone threaten to kill you just for existing?"

Both sisters exchange a knowing look.

Lana: "More than once."

Kellyn: "Yeah. Me too. And I don’t mean internet threats. I mean—corner-of-the-eye, in-the-street, real ones. Someone clocking you, realizing you don’t fit the template, and deciding that your existence is a personal offense to them.  And the wild part is—they’re not even really looking at you. They’re looking at a projection that threatens their idea of safety.  You can almost see the panic in their eyes. Like you accidentally reminded them that the script isn’t real."

Lilly (softly): "You just existing breaks the fourth wall."

Kellyn: "Exactly.  And they can’t stand it.  They have to push the world back into focus by erasing whatever’s blurry—whoever’s blurry.  I spent years internalizing that I was the mistake in the picture.  That the hostility was data I needed to fix myself around.  But now I think: the blur was the truth.  I was never the problem.  The problem was everyone’s need for the image to stay sharp."

Lana: "The same lie we built stories to dismantle.  I remember when we made The Matrix, how many people called it dangerous.  Not because it glorified rebellion, but because it suggested that rebellion might be necessary."

Lilly: "Or even possible.  The idea that reality could be wrong—that’s what terrifies people."

Kellyn looks between them, the flickering firelight from the camp catching in their eyes.

Kellyn: "I think that’s why strangers can get violent so fast.  It’s not just bigotry.  It’s existential terror.  You’re proof that their map isn’t the territory.  They’d rather destroy the reminder than redraw the map."

Lana (quietly): "And when you survive it, they call you brave."

Kellyn (laughing bitterly): "I hate that word.  I’m not brave.  I’m just alive.  I didn’t survive because I wanted to make a statement.  I survived because I wanted to make dinner the next day."

A pause. The creek gurgles softly.

Lilly: "That’s the most radical thing there is, though.  Not dying on schedule."

Kellyn (half-smiling): "Yeah.  I guess that’s the one rule I’ve always broken consistently."

Lana leans back, staring at the stars.

Lana: "I think about that all the time.  How the people who claim to love order will threaten absolute chaos just to preserve it.  But every act of hatred proves the thing they’re fighting isn’t wrong—it’s real."

Kellyn looks up too.  The stars tremble faintly in the rippling reflection of the water.

Kellyn: "I used to think the best revenge was proving them wrong.  Now I think it’s just…still being here.  Still getting to build worlds with people who don’t flinch when I breathe."

Lilly: "That’s not revenge.  That’s resurrection."

The Furby lets out a tiny mechanical coo, like punctuation.  All three laugh quietly.

Closing Image
The creek glimmers like film grain under starlight.  Three reflections ripple together—indistinct, uncontainable, perfectly blurred.

Fade out. End of Episode 6.

Season 111, Episode 7 — “When Survival Looks Like Defiance”

The morning mist hasn’t burned off yet.  The camp is quiet except for the occasional creak of branches and the soft shuffle of someone starting the fire.  Kellyn sits on a log near the edge of camp, wrapped in a blanket, holding a cup of tea that’s long gone cold.  Kyle approaches, hesitating for a second before sitting beside them.

They don’t speak right away.  The silence between them is familiar—gentle, not loaded.

Kyle: "You didn’t sleep much."

Kellyn: "No.  Too many flashbacks I didn’t ask for.  Some of them are old.  Some are just…echoes of yesterday."

Kyle: "Because of what you talked about with the Wachowskis?"

Kellyn: "Partly.  It opened a door I’ve been trying to keep closed for years.  It’s one thing to be sick—really sick—and have no one believe you.  But it’s another thing entirely when strangers see the adaptations that keep you alive and decide those are threats."

Kyle turns toward them fully, careful not to interrupt.

Kellyn: "I still remember this guy at a gas station once.  I was wearing a respirator mask before masks were normal.  I couldn't breathe otherwise.  He started shouting across the parking lot that people like me were ruining the country.  Said he’d rip it off my face himself if I came closer.  All because I needed oxygen.  Because my nervous system couldn’t handle his air."

Kyle: "Jesus."

Kellyn: "It happened all the time.  People saw my sunglasses and thought I was trying to look cool, not trying to keep from passing out.  Saw me sitting in my car outside a store and thought I was suspicious, not nursing a migraine.  Every adaptation was read as defiance.  Every attempt to survive looked like rebellion.  They’d rather I die quietly than disrupt the aesthetic of normal.  It wasn't even about going along to get along.  I wasn't disrupting acting but an aesthetic."

Kyle (quietly): "And no one ever stopped them."

Kellyn: "Of course not.  It’s hard to intervene when you secretly agree with the aggressor.  That’s what I learned.  Even the 'good ones' believed my survival came at their expense somehow.  Like their comfort was the tax on my existence."

Kyle rubs his palms together slowly, jaw tightening.

Kyle: "I used to think my job was to help people adapt to the world.  To make survival easier by helping them change how they reacted.  But the more you talk, the more I realize—the sickness isn’t in you.  It’s in the world that refuses to make room."

Kellyn: "Exactly.  The same people who said they wanted me to get better were the ones who made sure I never could.  Because for me to heal would require them to admit the system that props them up is sick.  And they’ll do anything not to see that."

Kyle (after a long pause): "I think that’s what makes you dangerous to them.  You’re proof that the system isn’t working.  That adaptation doesn’t mean compliance."

Kellyn looks down at their trembling hands, gripping the mug.

Kellyn: "Yeah. And they can’t stand that I figured out how to live without their permission.  So they call me difficult, delusional, manipulative—whatever word makes them feel like they’re not the villain."

Kyle’s throat tightens.

Kyle: "I can’t imagine living with that constant scrutiny."

Kellyn: "You’d be surprised what you can get used to.  Eventually, the fear stops feeling like fear.  It just becomes texture.  Part of the background noise.  Like the sound of wind through the trees—always there, whether you like it or not."

The wind picks up slightly as if to prove the point.  A long silence stretches between them.

Kyle: "When you talk about it now…do you still feel it in your body?"

Kellyn: "I cannot feel my body when I'm around other people.  What I expressed is the entire bodily signal compressed into one binary: Stay, or bolt."

Kyle exhales slowly, staring into the woods.

Kyle: "You’ve taught me more about trauma than any training ever did."

Kellyn: "Good.  Then teach that forward.  Not the symptoms—the system.  Make people see the way it kills us slowly and calls it our fault."

Kyle: (nodding) "I will."

The fire crackles faintly in the distance.  Kellyn finally takes a sip of the now-cold tea, grimacing at the taste.

Kellyn: "I have to admit, I do hate them sometimes—the ones who threaten.  The ones who stare.  The ones who think I’m the problem because I don’t look like the version of 'wellness' they recognize.  The irony is that the very adaptations they vilify me for are what would keep them from feeling so threatened by me."

They swirl the tea in the mug absently.

Kellyn: "I wear a respirator mask for my mental health too, not just my physical health.  Not every allergen triggers anaphylaxis.  Some trigger aggression.  I wear the mask to protect others as much as myself.  My biggest priority isn’t preventing MCAS symptoms for my own sake—it’s protecting others from what happens when I’m unwell around them."

Kyle (frowning): "You mean…you regulate yourself to prevent yourself from retaliating at the people who lash out at you?"

Kellyn: "Exactly.  Or from harming someone innocent.  And it’s not even theoretical.  When I first moved to Minnesota, I drove downtown one morning when I had too much brain fog to do it safely.  I caused an accident.  No one was hurt, but when the cops showed up, they arrested a young Black girl from the car I hit—for something completely unrelated.  They might never have found her if I hadn’t hit them."

Kellyn’s voice cracks slightly.

Kellyn: "I replay that every time I make a choice about how to move through the world.  I decided that day I would do everything in my power to keep my illness from harming anyone else ever again.  So yeah, I hate them—because I’ve built my whole life around not making the world worse for anyone, and still, they see me as a threat."

A long silence passes before Kyle speaks again.

Kyle: "That’s…almost unbearable, the way you’re forced to hold their comfort as a condition for your existence."

Kellyn: "Yeah.  And you know what’s crazy?  People liked me better when I was an unmitigated disaster.  When I was collapsing, panicking, reacting—it felt familiar to them.  They could pity it.  It didn’t challenge their sense of order.  But when I started taking steps to regulate myself—when I started wearing the mask, or setting boundaries, or avoiding triggers—they acted like I was doing something wrong.  Like I’d become arrogant for surviving in a way they didn’t understand."

Kyle: "Because your health threatened their worldview more than your illness ever did."

Kellyn: "Exactly.  Me getting better made them uncomfortable.  They’d rather see me falling apart—it fits the story they want to tell about themselves.  That they’re the sane, the balanced, the ones keeping the world in line.  Meanwhile, I’m the one sitting in the car crying over whether the air in a building might kill me, trying to figure out how to exist without causing collateral damage."

Kellyn’s expression hardens.

Kellyn: "That’s why I don’t reach out to people anymore.  They don’t want you to be the best version of yourself—they just want you to be the version that threatens them least.  They say they want the world to be better, but they throw a tantrum when you stop eating gluten so you’ll be less aggressive.  They’re addicted to the performance of caring, not the practice of it."

Kyle’s eyes glisten as he stares at the ground.

Kyle: "I used to tell myself I could help people change from the inside out.  But listening to you, I realize—it’s not them that’s rotten.  The system itself is built to reject people who prove it’s broken."

Kellyn: "That’s what I’ve been saying all along.  There’s nothing pathological about adapting to a hostile environment.  What’s pathological is the environment.  I can accept that the world is how it is—that things won't change overnight.  What I can't accept is when people demand I stop adapting as an individual to an untenable situation."

Kyle nods slowly, swallowing the lump in his throat.

Kyle: "You shouldn’t have to carry both sides of that—the pain of being targeted and the guilt of trying not to make anyone else pay for it."

Kellyn: "I know.  But the truth is, it’s not guilt anymore.  It’s just pattern recognition.  The system’s reflex is predictable.  I just don’t want to give it new data to weaponize."

They fall silent again.  A breeze moves through the trees, scattering ash from the fire.  Kellyn’s voice softens.

Kellyn: "I hate them sometimes.  But mostly, I pity them.  Because if you can’t stand to see someone else breathe differently, how small must your own lungs be?"

Kyle doesn’t answer.  He just reaches over and takes the mug from Kellyn’s shaking hands, refilling it from the thermos beside him.  The act is small, quiet, wordless—an answer that requires no permission and no translation.

Closing Image
Steam rises again from Kellyn’s cup, drifting upward in thin, wavering ribbons.  Not perfect, not still—but enough to warm their hands.

Fade out. End of Episode 7.

Season 111, Episode 8 — “The Wrong Place to Be Saved”

The sun is dipping low—amber through the cottonwoods, long shadows spilling across camp.  Kellyn and Kyle haven’t moved much since morning; they’re still by the firepit, a half-circle of ash and stone that’s grown warm again with the day’s heat.  Someone’s laughter echoes faintly from the distance, but here it’s quiet.

Kyle: "You’ve gone still again.  Still thinking about what you said earlier?"

Kellyn: "Yeah.  About the difference between what people say they want for you and what they can actually tolerate.  I keep thinking about how every single person who ever told me to 'just go to the ER' thought they were being helpful.  But the ER is the most dangerous place on earth for me."

Kyle looks up, startled by the certainty in their tone.

Kellyn: "I don’t mean that as an exaggeration.  If I walk into an emergency room, I will come out sicker—or not come out at all.  It’s not just that they don’t know what to do with someone like me.  It’s that they’ll actively make things worse trying to force my body to respond like theirs.  They’ll inject things I’ve already told them I’m allergic to, 'just to be sure.' They’ll gaslight me while I’m in anaphylaxis.  And afterward, they’ll write 'malingering' or 'drug-seeking' in the file because they don’t have a better story for what they can’t explain.  Every visit contaminates my record further—making the next one even deadlier."

His voice is low, cautious, almost like he’s afraid of stepping on something fragile.

Justin: “Can I ask you something kind of blunt?”

Kellyn: “Sure.”

Justin: “What’s it actually like for you when something happens—when your body goes into crisis—and you know we can’t really help?”

Kellyn takes a breath and looks at the river. Their voice is soft but certain.

Kellyn: “It’s honestly like a religious experience or a high.  Not numbness—more like being laser-focused.  The worse the symptoms are, the less fear and pain even register.  Every sound feels far away.  Think about the last time you threw up—nothing else exists in that moment, right?  Even if someone’s standing right there, they’re just…set dressing.”

Justin’s face creases a little, not with pity but understanding.

Kellyn: “Don’t get me wrong—it’s nice to have someone nearby.  But I don’t even notice how lonely it feels to be in that state until it’s already passed.  I don’t realize how close I got to the edge until it’s over.  I’ve survived so many things no one expected me to that I usually forget I even could die.  The worry isn’t ‘What if I die?’  It’s more like, ‘What if I get stuck in this state forever?’”

The words hang there, quiet as the current.

Justin: “That sounds like something between transcendence and terror.”

Kellyn (nods): “Exactly.  The body stops negotiating.  Everything unnecessary gets stripped away.  It’s pure presence, but involuntary.  You’re an inhabitant of yourself instead of a witness.”

Justin: “And after?”

Kellyn lets out a breath that almost sounds like a laugh.  “After comes peace.  The body has no more stress hormones to spend.  There is only sunlight and wind.  Gravity.”

Justin: “I don’t think I’ve ever experienced anything close to that.  But I get what you mean about gravity.  Music’s like that sometimes—when you’re playing and suddenly there’s no ‘you’ doing it anymore.  Then the song ends, and you crash back into your body.”

Kellyn looks over, smiling faintly. “That’s probably the closest most people get to it voluntarily.  Except my ‘encore’ usually involves nausea.”

Justin chuckles, quiet but genuine. “I’ll take applause over nausea any day.”

Kellyn: “To each their own, I guess.”

They fall silent again, the kind that feels easy, not heavy.  The river murmurs.  A bird darts low across the surface, wings skimming the water.

Justin: “So what would help, if there’s a next time?  I mean—not to fix it, but to not make it worse?”

Kellyn thinks for a long moment. “Be calm.  Stay close.  Don’t fill the space with words.  Just remind me I’m still here by being here yourself.  Everything else is just noise.”

Justin nods, slow and deliberate. “I can do that.”

Kellyn: “I know you can.”

Season 111, Episode 17 — “After the Edge”

The camp has gone still.  Even the river sounds hushed.  Inside the small shelter, Justin sits cross-legged with his guitar across his lap, notebook open beside him.
The lantern light pools in amber circles on the floor.

He hasn’t written in days.  But tonight, something hums behind his ribs — the residue of Kellyn’s words still echoing.

He speaks the first few lines aloud as he writes them, his voice almost a whisper.

"It’s not pain, it’s precision.  Not dying, but getting close enough to remember how living works.  Focus so sharp it erases fear.  Every sound a bell you can’t unring."

He pauses, looks toward the door flap, half expecting Kellyn to appear again, half relieved they’re resting.

Then he keeps writing.

"I used to think faith was for the people who believed the story would end well.  Now I think faith is for the ones who know it might not end at all, but sing anyway."

He strums once — open strings, no melody, just resonance.  The sound hangs in the air like breath that refuses to leave.

Justin (softly): “You said everything unnecessary gets stripped away.  Maybe that’s what songwriting is, too.  Maybe we’re just trying to learn how to die a little slower while the light’s still good.”

He writes again, hands moving faster now.

"If the body is a country, faith is the border you cross every time you remember you’re still here.  Love isn’t rescue.  It’s presence that doesn’t flinch.  The chorus is always just breathing."

He sets the pen down, exhales, and leans back against the wall.  The guitar slides a little against his chest, strings humming faintly against the wood.

Justin (quietly, to himself): “They said everything else is just noise.”

He plucks a single string — one clear, low note — lets it fade into the night.

The notebook lies open beside him, ink still wet.  Outside, the river keeps moving.
For once, he doesn’t need to finish the song.  Just being awake feels like enough.

Closing Image

Season 111, Episode 18 — “A Million Circles”

The rain has just stopped.  A thin mist hovers over the ground, catching the light of the coals.  The others have gone to bed, leaving Kellyn and Kyle alone with the quiet hum of the night.

Kellyn stares into the fire for a long time before speaking.

Kellyn: “Can I tell you something weird?  It’s about a guy I met once off a dating app.”

Kyle: “Sure.”

Kellyn rubs their palms together, as if warming a memory out of their skin.

Kellyn: “He kept pressuring me to meet in person.  It was late, raining, and he lived downtown.  I knew it wasn’t safe for me—too many triggers all stacked together.  But he wouldn’t let up.  So eventually, I went.”

Kyle: “Even though you knew it would hurt you.”

Kellyn: “Yeah.  I thought maybe I was just overreacting, like everyone always told me.  But when I got there—right when I hit the cluster of high-rises—I couldn’t see.  It wasn’t blindness, exactly.  The rain had stopped, but my windshield was still covered in droplets.  Each one was a fisheye lens reflecting everything—traffic lights, windows, signs.  The city split into a million tiny circles, each of them an eye looking back at me.  My brain couldn’t put it back together.  I couldn’t tell where the road ended or the sidewalk began.”

Kyle: “That sounds terrifying.”

Kellyn: “It was.  But what made it worse was how sure he was that I was lying.  He kept saying, ‘It’s easy—there’s a street on one side of the building and a parking lot on the other.’  But I couldn’t see the difference.  It was all covered in eyes.  I tried to explain it, but he couldn’t imagine what it would be like not to see a unified scene.  To him, reality was obvious.”

They pause, staring at the smoke curling upward.

Kellyn: “That’s the part that always gets me about able-bodied people.  They never even learn that what they call reality is just an invention their brain made—a gestalt.  They think they’re perceiving the world, but really, they’re just smoothing it out so they don’t have to see the chaos.  When that smoothing function breaks, people can't believe it's real.  They can't believe that someone could look at a windshield covered in water droplets and see water droplets."

Kyle: “And that’s how it feels to look at people, too?”

Kellyn: “Exactly.  It’s like the same mechanism broke in my mind.  Everyone else says they see a person, a whole.  I see fragments—micro-expressions, tones, gestures, reflections—all orbiting each other but never collapsing into one thing.  It’s like trying to love a constellation when everyone else insists it’s a single star.  It's not a single star, and I'm not defective for refusing to enable their delusions."

The fire pops, sending a spray of sparks upward.  Kyle watches them fade and says quietly:

Kyle: “That sounds…exhausting.  And lonely.”

Kellyn: “Yeah.  Not the kind of lonely where no one’s around, but the kind where everyone insists you’re all looking at the same picture, and you know their picture doesn't exist outside their own mind.  They treat my illness as a delusion, when in reality, every single second of their experience is a delusion. But try telling them that!"

They lean back, closing their eyes.  "I’ve spent my whole life pretending to see what everyone else sees.  It’s the only way not to be harassed to death.  But I can't pretend I'm the one being disabled anymore.  I'm right.  They're wrong.  It's just that there are more of them, so they're louder."

Kyle doesn’t try to fix it.  He just sits closer, letting the silence thicken until it feels like company.

After a while, Kellyn opens their eyes again.

Kellyn: “You know what the worst part is?  The circles were beautiful.  I couldn’t see the world, but I could see light itself.  Which is all anyone ever actually sees.  They're just too scared to admit it."

Kyle: “I think you're right."

Kellyn nods, gaze still on the coals.  “That helps.”

Closing Image

Season 111, Episode 19 — “Fragments and Light”

The fire’s gone down to embers.  Kellyn has gone to sleep.  The others’ shelters breathe faintly in the dark — a cough here, a shifting blanket there — but otherwise, it’s just Kyle and the sound of water dripping from the eaves.

He stays where he is, knees drawn up, notebook open but untouched.  The page looks too clean for what he wants to write.  He sets the pen down and just breathes for a while.

Kyle (quietly, to himself): “A million circles…and everyone else calls it a view.”

He finally writes that down.  The ink beads slightly in the humidity.  Then he keeps going, words tumbling unfiltered:

"They said the windshield turned the whole city into light — and no one believed them.  Maybe that’s the real fracture: not the perception, but the disbelief.

I’ve spent my whole life building frameworks for seeing.  Diagnoses, developmental stages, adaptive strategies — all neat boxes for chaos.  But they're right.  All those boxes are just the mind pretending it knows where the street ends and the parking lot begins.  They're not lost in the rain.  We are."

He looks up, watching the faint glow of the coals fade and brighten.

Kyle (thinking): “How much of therapy is just convincing people to believe in the gestalt again — to agree on a shared hallucination so they can function inside it?”

He picks up the pen again, slower this time.

"Maybe healing isn’t about seeing the world clearly.  Maybe it’s about staying steady when it breaks apart.  They see fragments, not forms.  But maybe that’s closer to truth.  The rest of us just call it distortion because we can’t stand the loneliness of the real."

He leans back, the notebook resting on his thigh.  The night hums low around him — cicadas, water, his own pulse in his ears.

Kyle (whispering): “If I could see like them, would I still know who I am?”

He closes the notebook.  The question stays open.

For a long while he doesn’t move.
The mist starts to lift, and the last ember catches a raindrop sliding down from the tarp — a brief glint of color, one tiny circle of firelight.

Kyle watches it fall and disappear.

Closing Image

Season 111, Episode 20 — “The Furbic Oracle”

Morning breaks strangely.  The fog hasn’t lifted yet; it hangs low across the camp like breath that hasn’t decided whether to stay or leave.  Everyone’s just starting to stir when the Furby, sitting upright near the ashes of last night’s fire, flickers to life.

Its plastic eyes pulse. Its mouth clacks.

Furby (in that warped, tinny voice): “Aaron.  Aaron.  Aaron.  Aaron.”

Everyone freezes.  Kellyn, half-asleep and still tangled in their blanket, lifts their head.

Kellyn: “Is Aaron okay?"

Jonathan (checking): “Yeah.  I’ll go make sure he didn’t wander off in the night.”

Jonathan: “He’s fine! Still asleep.  Snoring.”

Furby: “Dessner.  Dessner.  Dessner.  Dessner.”

The camp goes silent again.  Even the fennec foxes, lurking at the treeline, stop moving.

Justin (deadpan): “Does…it mean Aaron Dessner?”

Furby: “Both!  Both!  Both!”

Kyle: “Maybe it’s just glitching.”

Matt: “Or maybe the Furby is, you know, clairvoyant again.”

Harry: “God help us if it’s both.”

The Search

Back at the Fire
